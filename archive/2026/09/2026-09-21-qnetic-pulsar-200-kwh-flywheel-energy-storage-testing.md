# 200-kWh Flywheel Enters Full-Scale Testing as Qnetic Targets Data Centers and Grid Storage

**Published:** 2026-09-21
**Live article:** https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/21/qnetic-pulsar-200-kwh-flywheel-energy-storage-testing/

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A mechanical energy-storage technology designed around a rapidly spinning mass rather than electrochemical battery cells has reached a new testing milestone.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

Full-scale flywheel energy storage concept for grid and data-center power infrastructure.</figcaption></figure><!-- /wp:image -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>On September 21, 2026, Qnetic announced that its <strong>Pulsar 200-kWh flywheel energy-storage prototype</strong> has begun low-speed integrated testing. The company says the program is validating its control, safety and power-electronics systems before progressively increasing rotational speed.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>According to Qnetic, Pulsar has completed its first <strong>100 continuous charge-discharge cycles with zero faults or capacity degradation</strong>. Those are company-reported early test results rather than proof of long-term commercial performance. Qnetic also reports that its separately tested motor produced 130 kW at 97.3% efficiency and exceeded 11,000 rpm.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">A Battery That Stores Energy as Motion</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Flywheels work differently from lithium-ion batteries. Electricity drives a motor that accelerates a rotor, storing energy as rotational kinetic energy. When electricity is needed again, the system reverses the process and uses the spinning rotor to drive electrical generation.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Qnetic says Pulsar combines composite materials, magnetic bearings and power electronics. Its development targets include rapid response, high cycle counts and reduced performance degradation over a long operating life. Those long-term performance targets still require validation.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Data Centers Matter</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Qnetic identifies <strong>AI data centers, renewable-energy integration, grid balancing, microgrids and industrial power resilience</strong> as potential applications. Modern data centers can place large and rapidly changing loads on electrical infrastructure. Storage close to the load can potentially absorb short-duration fluctuations, provide ride-through capability or complement other backup-power systems.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Next Test Is More Important Than the First 100 Cycles</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Pulsar has not yet demonstrated all of Qnetic's long-term performance claims. The company says data from the prototype will be shared with the <strong>Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI)</strong> through its deRISKED program. Qnetic entered that evaluation with sponsorship from Sacramento Municipal Utility District.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Full-speed operation and longer-duration testing should provide stronger evidence about efficiency, reliability, thermal behavior and degradation. A prototype completing 100 cycles without a fault is an engineering milestone; it is not the same as demonstrating years of operation in a commercial data center or electrical grid.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The important development is that Qnetic has moved from assembly and component validation into integrated operation of a full-scale 200-kWh machine. For a technology built around repeatedly storing electricity as physical motion, the next measurements—<strong>how efficiently it cycles, how reliably it operates and how its performance changes with time</strong>—will determine whether the flywheel becomes a practical alternative or complement to conventional batteries.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
