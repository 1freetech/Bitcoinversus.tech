Researchers at Northwestern University and Argonne National Laboratory have developed an electrically controlled fabric that captured approximately 2.6 times as much phosphate as an unpowered comparison in laboratory tests. Their reusable material combines ordinary carbon cloth with magnetite nanoparticles, and a change in voltage helps release the collected nutrient for potential recovery.

Published November 18, 2026 in ACS ES&T Engineering, the research explores a dual problem. Excess phosphorus from fertilizer runoff can promote harmful algal blooms and deplete oxygen in waterways. Phosphate is also a finite, economically valuable fertilizer input. Recovering it rather than discarding it could eventually address pollution and resource supply together. Northwestern's announcement describes a laboratory advance, not an installed municipal treatment system.

A carbon cloth coated with iron oxide

Graduate researcher Kelly Matuszewski and collaborators cleaned conductive carbon fabric, then repeatedly dipped it into a suspension of magnetite nanoparticles. Magnetite, a form of iron oxide, provides sites that can attract phosphate. The cloth's conductive fibers turn the resulting material into an electrode capable of participating in an electrically driven separation process.

In the team's hybrid capacitive deionization experiment, applying 1.2 volts across the electrochemical cell increased phosphate capture by approximately 2.6-fold relative to the no-voltage control. Changing the electrical polarity encouraged phosphate release back into solution, creating a potential recovery stage. Unlike some conventional approaches, the release step did not require adding a chemical regenerant.

The 2.6-fold improvement is a measured relative comparison within the researchers' experimental conditions. It does not mean the cloth removed 260% of phosphate, made water safe to drink, or operated 2.6 times faster. The published university summary does not establish a universal capture percentage, a plant-scale electricity requirement, or an operating cost per kilogram recovered.

Why wastewater plants might care

Traditional phosphate treatment can involve dosing water with iron or aluminum salts, followed by solid separation and sludge handling. Chemical consumption, pH changes, and waste disposal complicate treatment economics. An electrical capture-and-release cycle could offer more control over when nutrients are collected and concentrated, although actual efficiency will depend on water chemistry, electrode longevity, throughput, and the cost of electricity.

The hard part comes after the laboratory

Real wastewater contains competing ions, organic material, suspended solids, and microorganisms. Their interactions may alter phosphate uptake, contaminate electrodes, or reduce efficiency after repeated cycles. Researchers still need to test actual wastewater matrices and determine how the cloth behaves under continuous flow, with realistic fouling and maintenance requirements.

The work also has commercial context. Northwestern disclosed that Matuszewski and Dravid are inventors on a provisional patent application related to the research. Dravid is a founder, equity holder, and board director of Coral Innovations, a company developing water-treatment technologies. Those connections do not invalidate the laboratory results, but independent engineering replication and transparent costs will matter before broad commercial claims can be evaluated.

For now, the most defensible conclusion is narrow and useful: a voltage-controlled, magnetite-coated fabric demonstrated stronger phosphate capture than an unpowered control and reversible release in laboratory experiments. Whether the approach can recover fertilizer economically while protecting waterways remains an open engineering question.
