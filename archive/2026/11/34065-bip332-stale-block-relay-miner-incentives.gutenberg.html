Bitcoin developers are debating whether faster circulation of losing blocks could slightly change the economics of mining. Bitcoin Optech’s November 20, 2026 newsletter describes renewed discussion of BIP 332, a draft for sharing recent stale block information across the peer-to-peer network. The dispute concerns a small modeled benefit for large mining pools, not a newly detected attack or an approved change to Bitcoin’s consensus rules.

A stale block is a valid block that loses a race against a competing chain with greater cumulative proof of work. Nodes ordinarily focus on the active chain, so losing blocks can be difficult for researchers to observe. Sharing their headers could help operators distinguish natural propagation delays from network partitions or strategically withheld blocks.

What would BIP 332 actually send?

The draft describes an optional staletip message containing a known fork-point hash, compact headers from the losing branch and a flag indicating whether full block data is available. Each compressed header occupies 48 bytes, excluding the previous block hash. Participating peers would first negotiate support through BIP 434.

Suggested limits include up to 20 headers in an announcement, a 1,000-block recency window and at most ten previously known tips advertised when a peer connects. Those restrictions aim to limit bandwidth and resource-exhaustion risks. The feature is optional; its publication as a BIP draft does not mean Bitcoin nodes already relay stale blocks under a network-wide rule.

The 0.26 BTC question

Developer Anthony Towns modeled a mining pool controlling 20% of global hashrate that briefly continues working on its own block after learning another pool found a competing block at the same height. Under the assumptions in his November discussion, the strategy already creates an estimated 16.1 BTC in annual expected benefit compared with immediately switching to the competing block.

Shortening a hypothetical one-block reorganization relay window from five seconds to one raises the estimate to 16.36 BTC per year, a difference of only 0.26 BTC annually. The calculation also assumes workers fetch new jobs around every three seconds. These are model outputs for a particular strategic choice, not measured profit at a named pool or additional revenue for all miners.

The 0.26 BTC increase is about 1.6% of the modeled 16.1 BTC advantage, not 1.6% of a pool’s total mining revenue. Pool payout arrangements also change who receives the benefit. Smaller pools, different work-update intervals and different network delays produce different results.

Why developers disagree over full block data

Antoine Poinsot suggested beginning with header-only relay. Headers improve visibility into competing chains without necessarily speeding a later reorganization as much as delivering complete blocks. Faster full-block availability could modestly improve the expected return of miners who persist with their own losing block, although it can also help honest nodes converge on the best chain more quickly.

Towns argued that miners with a material incentive can already deploy private relay infrastructure. Full stale-block contents could also help observers assess whether a suspected reorganization involved transaction censorship or delayed publication, instead of relying on a block header alone. Developers have not reached a final implementation agreement, and neither argument establishes that the proposed relay increases network-wide attack profitability.

What operators should monitor

The site previously documented three one-block reorganizations in four weeks. Individual block races do not prove malicious behavior. A more comprehensive stale-block record could help interpret comparable events, but any monitor remains subject to its network connections and collection gaps.

OCEAN’s analysis of a contested chain, the Stratum mining protocol and new decentralized-payout research concern related but distinct parts of mining. Block propagation governs how quickly nodes learn about competing work; Stratum coordinates miner jobs, while payout protocols decide how rewards reach participants.

Before deployment, BIP 332 needs review of resource limits, privacy exposure, denial-of-service protections, stale data availability and real-world propagation performance. The immediate development is a public technical debate over transparency and incentives, with a small quantified tradeoff worth investigating rather than a confirmed network upgrade.
