## Original published Gutenberg markup

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>After nearly eight years in space, an X-ray telescope aboard <strong>BepiColombo</strong> has captured its first astronomical images. Instead of photographing Mercury, the UK-led <strong>Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS)</strong> aimed at the <strong>Crab Nebula, approximately 6,500 light-years away</strong>, to check whether its optics and detector still work. The <a href="https://le.ac.uk/news/2026/november/first-x-ray-imaging-instrument-planet-first-picture">University of Leicester released the results November 20, 2026</a>, one day before the spacecraft's planned Mercury orbit insertion.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Researchers combined <strong>39 hours of observations from September 11–12</strong>, including 55 spacecraft pointing maneuvers. The calibration showed slightly better angular resolution than MIXS achieved during its last laboratory measurements in 2015. The work represents a successful in-space optical test, not a chemical map of Mercury or a newly discovered cosmic object.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><em>The University of Leicester links to the MIXS video from its official November 20 first-light announcement. The footage explains the instrument and observations.</em> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A839XzbGXvc" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Original video</a>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why an instrument headed for Mercury looked at a supernova remnant</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The Crab Nebula is a familiar X-ray source produced by a stellar explosion observed from Earth in 1054. Its well-studied emission lets engineers evaluate whether a telescope is sharp, correctly aligned and stable. In the MIXS test, researchers measured how widely incoming X-ray photons spread across the image sensor. The narrower the detected spread, the better the telescope can distinguish neighboring features on Mercury.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:image {"id":34102,"sizeSlug":"full","linkDestination":"none"} --><figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img src="https://bitcoinversus.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/bepicolombo-mixs-t-crab-nebula-first-light-september-2026.jpg" alt="Actual MIXS-T first light Crab Nebula image showing X-ray detected photon rate, stellar background, sky coordinates and the target inset" class="wp-image-34102" /><figcaption><strong>Actual BepiColombo MIXS-T calibration image.</strong> X-ray photons from the Crab Nebula overlaid on a visible-light star field, September 11–12, 2026. Source: <a href="https://le.ac.uk/news/2026/november/first-x-ray-imaging-instrument-planet-first-picture">University of Leicester MIXS team</a>; Digitized Sky Survey 2 reference background, published via Phys.org. Not an image of Mercury's surface.</figcaption></figure><!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The result is particularly meaningful because MIXS spent much of the journey unable to look into open space. Following the September 3 separation of the Mercury Transfer Module, its field of view became available for calibration. According to principal investigator <strong>Emma Bunce</strong>, the instrument shows no evidence of radiation damage after eight years. Cosmic-ray signals can be filtered, while some illumination-related fluorescence still requires study and optimization.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">An X-ray map of Mercury's chemistry</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>MIXS is not simply another camera. When energetic solar X-rays strike Mercury's surface, atoms in its rocks emit characteristic fluorescent X-rays. MIXS can record their energies to infer which elements are present. A companion instrument, the <strong>Solar Intensity X-ray and particle Spectrometer (SIXS)</strong>, monitors the incoming radiation so scientists can interpret the surface response rather than confuse solar activity with changes in rock composition. <a href="https://le.ac.uk/bepicolombo/mixs">Leicester's instrument specifications</a> list a mass of 10.8 kilograms and nominal power demand of 24 watts.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:image {"id":34104,"sizeSlug":"full","linkDestination":"none"} --><figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img src="https://bitcoinversus.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/leicester-bepicolombo-mixs-instrument-before-launch.jpg" alt="Authentic University of Leicester image showing the pair of MIXS X-ray optics assemblies in a cleanroom before the BepiColombo mission launch" class="wp-image-34104" /><figcaption><strong>Flight-instrument engineering photograph.</strong> The MIXS optical assemblies before BepiColombo launched in October 2018. Credit: University of Leicester, <a href="https://phys.org/news/2026-11-mercury-bound-ray-telescope-images.html">as published by Phys.org</a>. This is not the new Crab Nebula data image.</figcaption></figure><!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>MIXS is described by its developers as the first imaging X-ray telescope flown on a planetary science mission. The technology is intended to generate a global map of elemental composition, helping researchers investigate Mercury's formation and evolution. The significance differs from <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/18/nasa-nicer-xray-telescope-science-operations-end-2026/">NASA's NICER X-ray observatory</a>, which studied distant astrophysical objects rather than using solar-triggered fluorescence to map another planet.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

It predates the new MIXS results.</em> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WJqWGdWERqk" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Original video</a>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The next test is at Mercury</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>BepiColombo's combined European and Japanese orbiters were scheduled to enter Mercury orbit on <strong>November 21</strong>. The mission plan calls for the Mercury Planetary Orbiter and JAXA's Mio to separate on <strong>December 9–10</strong>, with science observations beginning in <strong>April 2027</strong>. Our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/18/bepicolombo-mercury-orbit-insertion-21-november-2026-esa-jaxa/">earlier arrival preview</a> details the demanding orbital maneuver; neither orbital insertion nor the full scientific campaign was completed when Leicester released the images.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The first-light image is an engineering milestone, not proof that every future surface measurement will meet mission requirements. Detector optimization, orbital operations, calibration against variable solar X-rays and interference from Mercury's space environment remain critical. Related work, including <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/18/nasa-webb-bao-stellar-merger-andromeda-supergiant-2026/">James Webb observations of an Andromeda stellar merger</a> and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/19/nasa-ucla-12-dust-grains-ancient-kuiper-belt-ice-oxygen-isotopes-2026/">analyses of microscopic dust beyond Neptune</a>, underscores how spacecraft sensors can turn faint radiation and tiny materials into evidence about planetary history.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
