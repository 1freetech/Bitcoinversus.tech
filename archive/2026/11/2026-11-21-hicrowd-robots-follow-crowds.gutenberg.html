A crowded station presents a problem for many delivery and guide robots: avoiding every nearby pedestrian can leave no acceptable route. Researchers at Sweden’s Örebro University have developed HiCrowd, a navigation system that follows compatible pedestrian flows instead of repeatedly stopping. A November 17, 2026 university report describes field demonstrations at Tokyo’s Miraikan science museum and Expo 2025 in Osaka.

A robot that reads pedestrian movement

The approach targets the “freezing robot problem,” in which a machine has no obvious safe opening through dense foot traffic. Humans often solve crowded walkways by moving with nearby groups. HiCrowd tries to learn comparable behavior, without assuming every person is a fixed obstacle.

The core software combines two planning layers. A reinforcement-learning policy chooses a follow point associated with a suitable pedestrian group, while model-predictive control calculates short-horizon movements around nearby people. The high-level layer anticipates where traffic may lead; the lower-level controller keeps the motion dynamically feasible.

University researcher Yufei Zhu also developed “Maps of Dynamics,” models of how people typically move through a particular location and at different times. The goal is not mind reading: observations of positions and velocities support predictions about local movement patterns. Any deployment in public areas still needs sensible privacy, accessibility and safety controls.

What the performance results actually show

HiCrowd’s peer-reviewed ICRA 2026 paper tested its method against conventional and learning-based navigation planners. In the offline ETH-UCY replay benchmark, HiCrowd completed 88% of navigation episodes, compared with 76% for pure MPC, 61% for ORCA, 55% for CrowdAttn and 24% for SARL. Pedestrians in the recorded trajectories could not react to the robot, so the benchmark is not equivalent to a live crowd.

The same offline benchmark reported average navigation times of 21.82 seconds for HiCrowd and 40.65 seconds for MPC, even though HiCrowd sometimes takes a longer physical route. Following traffic can beat a seemingly shorter path if an oncoming group would otherwise force repeated stops. The original authors also tested scenarios in which simulated pedestrians could respond to the machine.

Real-world testing, with human safeguards

Real-world trials add evidence, but impose important qualifications. The paper documents 20 museum trial runs with no significant observed collisions or freezing. Its suitcase-sized research platform used a front stereo camera, a lidar sensor and a computer carrying an Intel Core i7 processor and NVIDIA RTX 2070 GPU; speed was capped at 1 meter per second.

Researchers also required a human supervisor to hold a touch-sensitive handle for autonomous movement, according to the experimental methods. The team selected a broad space with relatively few fixed obstacles. Consequently, a public demonstration does not establish that an unsupervised robot can guide someone safely through every crowded subway, escalator or emergency evacuation.

The possible payoff is broader than convenience. A future assistive navigation robot might help someone with a visual impairment move naturally through stations or airports. The university positions that application as future potential, not an approved guide-dog replacement or shipping consumer product. Existing airport robotics trials illustrate why busy public facilities are a demanding proving ground.

The research also fits alongside human-guided warehouse robots, reinforcement learning on heavy machinery, and secure connectivity for mobile robots. Successful deployments will depend on reliable perception, safeguards around nearby people, transparent testing and practical human oversight.

Why following people could matter

HiCrowd’s most interesting idea is counterintuitive: navigating well may require accepting a detour and moving alongside other people, rather than always chasing the shortest empty path. The study provides a measurable benchmark improvement and a promising supervised field demonstration, while leaving certification and large-scale assisted travel as future challenges.
