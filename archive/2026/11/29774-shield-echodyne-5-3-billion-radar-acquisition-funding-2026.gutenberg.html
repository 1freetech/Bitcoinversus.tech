Shield announced November 5, 2026, an agreement to acquire radar manufacturer Echodyne for up to $5.3 billion, combining autonomous-aircraft software with sensors used to detect drones. Shield also outlined a $2.5 billion financing package linked to the purchase. The companies expect closing in early 2027, pending regulatory approvals and other conditions. The acquisition has been agreed, not completed.

The terms include $4.8 billion payable in cash and equity at closing plus as much as $500 million contingent on Echodyne reaching performance targets. Shield plans a $1 billion Series H round at a $28.8 billion post-money valuation, another $1 billion of structured equity from Blackstone-managed funds and $500 million in debt led by JPMorgan. Bloomberg reports the financing depends on the acquisition closing.

The business behind Echodyne’s radar

Based in Kirkland, Washington, Echodyne develops compact, software-defined radars using its proprietary Metamaterials Electronically Scanned Array, or MESA, architecture. Electronic beam steering and sensor processing help operators locate and track small aircraft. Echodyne says its devices are used in counter-drone and security systems across more than 40 countries, including civilian airport and stadium protection.

Echodyne opened an 86,350-square-foot Washington manufacturing facility in July after a $40 million investment. Its planned capacity exceeds 30,000 radar systems annually. That is a production-capability estimate, not a disclosed shipment total. The company expects to retain its product lines, engineering team and independent customer relationships after the deal.

Building a larger autonomy business

Shield develops Hivemind autonomy software, V-BAT surveillance aircraft and the X-BAT program. Acquiring Echodyne would add a business that sells sensing hardware to military, public-safety and commercial customers. The deal could widen Shield’s addressable market without demonstrating immediate sales growth. The companies forecast triple-digit revenue growth in their current fiscal years but did not provide comparable revenue figures in the announcement.

The broader sensor market already includes Lantronix’s autonomous monitoring equipment and Applied Energetics’ reported counter-drone laser development. Those products perform different functions; detection, classification and any subsequent intervention must be evaluated as separate capabilities.

Orders, approvals and integration risks

Echodyne’s September procurement agreement with a U.S. counter-drone task force has a $250 million contract ceiling over an ordering period of up to 36 months. A ceiling is the maximum potential procurement value, not booked revenue or guaranteed orders. Manufacturing capacity also does not establish utilization, delivery schedules or customer adoption.

Shield must obtain regulatory clearance, close the financing and preserve Echodyne’s relationships with third-party integrators. GeekWire reports the radar maker employs about 400 people and that no layoffs are currently planned. Those plans can change, and post-acquisition integration may involve additional engineering and commercial costs.

The deal reflects a broader shift toward defense-oriented autonomy, also visible in Saronic’s autonomous-vessel program, Flock Safety’s emergency-response drones and autonomous fleet economics. The decisive next evidence will be transaction closing, verified orders and sustained deliveries—not the headline valuation alone.
