An older Bitcoin miner may soon have an easier path away from factory firmware. On October 1, 2026, the nonprofit 256 Foundation said it had extended funding for Mujina, an open-source mining firmware project, through December. Its immediate goal is practical: prepare installable operating-system images and an installer for Antminer S19j Pro and S19k Pro hardware. Neither a generally available installer nor completed fleet validation was announced.

Why firmware matters

An ASIC hashboard performs SHA-256 calculations, but its controller needs software to assign chip frequencies, manage operating conditions, submit work to a pool and report faults. An owner can replace a fan or repair a board without gaining comparable control over the manufacturer's software. BitcoinVersus.tech previously examined how ASIC control boards operate and the hashboard's electrical architecture.

Mujina is written in Rust for Linux and publishes its source code. Developers aim to make chip control auditable and adaptable to additional hardware models. Public code permits inspection and independent modifications, but code availability alone cannot establish security, sustained uptime or compatibility with every controller revision.

The installer is the missing piece

The Foundation initially funded work from April through September 2026, then extended the term for three months. Its October roadmap lists S19j Pro and S19k Pro testing, documentation, flashable images and a user-facing installer. The same announcement describes Stratum V2, Intel BZM2 chip support and other changes as work awaiting review rather than merged, shipping features.

The project's published compatibility guide, updated in July, described S19 support as a prototype fork without ready-to-install images. The newer funding announcement sets a delivery objective; it is not independent proof that every promised release has landed. Operators should check current source, releases and hardware support before attempting a flash.

Real hardware, with important limits

The Foundation documented a demonstration using one S19j Pro hashboard and two S19k Pro hashboards on one control board. Its reported dashboard showed 280 chips and 136.70 TH/s total. That mixed-generation bench result is technically important because component-level replacement may extend a machine's useful life. Yet a published demonstration is not a certified production benchmark, a measured efficiency comparison or proof of long-term reliability.

Swiss developer RY3T also described its Nova solar-mining project as able to follow changing power targets from about 1,000 to 3,000 watts within seconds. The announced design can coordinate up to three miners for a 9 kW target. Those figures come from a developer demonstration and product plan, not an independently tested commercial fleet.

What miners should check first

Firmware freedom has operational costs. An incompatible image or failed flash can immobilize a controller, while untested tuning can raise temperatures, rejected-share rates and power consumption. BitcoinVersus.tech's firmware security lesson covers recovery planning; its Stratum guide explains pool-protocol differences. A responsible rollout would preserve stock recovery images, verify download hashes and run staged power and stability tests.

Mujina's significance is therefore less about claiming faster hashrate than giving ASIC owners a credible, inspectable control layer. The next evidence to watch is a downloadable S19 image, documented supported revisions, repeatable install and recovery procedures, and sustained wall-power and accepted-share measurements. Until those arrive, a promising open-source milestone remains a development milestone.
