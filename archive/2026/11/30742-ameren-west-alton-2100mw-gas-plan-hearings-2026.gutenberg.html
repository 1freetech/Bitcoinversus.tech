Ameren Missouri's proposed 2,100-megawatt West Alton Energy Center remains unapproved, according to the utility's quarterly filing published November 6, 2026. The company still targets the fourth quarter of 2031 for service. Missouri regulators have scheduled public hearings for November 18 and 19, bringing a major gas-power proposal before customers as data-center electricity demand grows. West Alton is planned generation, not 2.1 GW already operating.

The utility first announced the proposed facility in July and filed its certificate application on July 24. It would sit beside the existing Sioux Energy Center in St. Charles County, Missouri. The Missouri Public Service Commission describes three advanced-class combined-cycle units. The November filing records the authorization as filed rather than approved, and Ameren expects a decision in the first half of 2027.

How the proposed gas station would work

Combined-cycle generation captures more of a fuel's available energy than a simple-cycle turbine. Natural gas and compressed air burn inside a combustion turbine, spinning a generator. Its hot exhaust passes through a heat-recovery steam generator to produce steam, which drives another turbine. The process produces electricity twice from the same fuel flow, although actual efficiency depends on equipment, ambient conditions and loading.

Why Ameren expects demand to rise

Ameren's September integrated resource plan projects adding 1,900 MW of simple-cycle gas by 2029 and 2,100 MW of combined-cycle gas by 2031, alongside 2,400 MW of battery storage and 1,300 MW of solar by 2030. Those are planning figures with different operating roles, not a single fleet already producing electricity. Ameren's November filing identifies roughly 2.8 GW of signed large-customer load agreements, expected to start affecting demand in the second half of 2027 and reach full levels by 2030.

The changing demand outlook connects energy infrastructure with the state's data-center development. Signed electricity agreements do not prove facilities are built or permanently drawing their contracted capacity.

Customer costs, fuel and emissions

Supporters argue a dispatchable combined-cycle station would replace aging generation and provide power during heat waves, winter peaks and lower renewable output. Ameren says using Sioux's established site could reduce some connection costs and create more than 1,000 construction jobs. Yet fuel purchases, turbine availability, cooling-water requirements and transmission arrangements still matter to delivered electricity prices and reliability.

A new gas station would also emit carbon dioxide when operating, and methane leakage in the fuel supply chain adds climate concerns. Conventional gas generation can have lower direct emissions per megawatt-hour than older coal units without becoming carbon-free. Long-lived capital costs could be exposed to future electricity-market, environmental and regulatory changes. Ameren seeks permission for construction-work-in-progress cost recovery; regulators, not the company alone, determine which charges may enter customer bills.

Public hearings and the next decision

The commission lists an in-person hearing at St. Charles Community College in Cottleville on November 18 at 6 p.m., followed by a virtual hearing on November 19 at 6 p.m. The next decisive evidence will be the commission's certificate ruling, updated financing and construction milestones. Until then, West Alton belongs on a proposed-project map, not in an inventory of operating gigawatt-scale power plants.
