A solo Bitcoin miner found block 976,642 on November 19, 2026, collecting a gross block reward of 3.13932037 BTC through Solo CKPool. The block was confirmed at 22:04:57 UTC. Unlike many solo-mining headlines built around anonymous lottery odds, the operator disclosed useful hashrate measurements: approximately 471 terahashes per second (TH/s) across the account, including a winning worker reporting around 95 TH/s.

Bitcoin mining requires specialized ASIC computers to perform repeated SHA-256 hashing until a block header falls below the network target. A solo miner competes for the entire block reward rather than collecting a proportional payout from pooled mining. The pool operator’s November 19 confirmation and worker logs link the successful worker to the block, while the public block record independently establishes its timestamp, difficulty and reward.

What the winning miner actually ran

CKPool administrator Con Kolivas reported a seven-day account rate of roughly 470 TH/s and a one-day rate of 471 TH/s. The worker identified as “jacobi” showed about 95.4 TH/s over the preceding day. Those figures are pool-reported estimates based on submitted shares, not laboratory power measurements or a verified inventory of machines.

The record does not establish the ASIC manufacturer, number of physical units, electricity tariff or whether rented hashrate was involved. One worker name can represent more than one physical miner. Earlier October solo wins provide context, but cannot be used to identify the November hardware.

A roughly 39-year expectation, not a 39-year wait

At block 976,642, difficulty was approximately 134.82 trillion. Holding an individual hashrate of 471 TH/s and the difficulty constant gives an average expected wait of about 39 years. The probability of finding at least one block in one 365.25-day year is about 2.53%. Random outcomes do not follow the average: miners can win quickly, find several blocks or never win.

The model uses probability = 1 − exp[−(hashrate × seconds) ÷ (difficulty × 2³²)]. The 6 TH/s example is a hypothetical home miner, not the winning device. Real probabilities change with network difficulty, interruptions and a miner’s effective hashrate. November’s 9.13% difficulty reduction altered the starting conditions for every participant.

Why gross reward and miner payout differ

The verified block contained 3.125 BTC in subsidy and 0.01432037 BTC in transaction fees, across 1,003 transactions. Those amounts sum to 3.13932037 BTC before any service charge. Solo CKPool advertises a 2% fee; consequently, the entire gross reward should not be described as a fee-free payout to the winner. Exact distribution requires reading the coinbase outputs.

The event illustrates Bitcoin’s probabilistic mining design rather than a newly efficient machine. Even with large conventional pools dominating block production, independent participants can still win a block through a solo service. Whether doing so makes economic sense depends on power costs, hardware depreciation, network difficulty and how long the operator can tolerate zero revenue.
