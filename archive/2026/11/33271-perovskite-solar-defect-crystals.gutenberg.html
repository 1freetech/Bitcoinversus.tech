Scientists working across Princeton University, UC San Diego, Sungkyunkwan University and U.S. national laboratories have reported a chemical method for producing perovskite solar cells with fewer crystal defects. Brookhaven National Laboratory highlighted the findings on November 18, 2026, following publication in Science on October 8. The method uses a mixture of zinc bromide and methylammonium chloride during crystal formation. The peer-reviewed study reports 25.9% independently certified power conversion efficiency for a small cell and 23.9% efficiency for perovskite minimodules measuring more than 15 square centimeters. Those figures are laboratory results, not proof of commercially deployed panels.

Why a chemical mixture changes crystal growth

Perovskites are semiconducting crystals that absorb sunlight and generate mobile electric charge. Thin films can be deposited from solution, but rapid crystal growth may leave grain boundaries, compositional variations and atomic defects. Electrons and holes that recombine through defects lose energy that might otherwise contribute to electrical output. Defect control is therefore a central obstacle to efficient, durable photovoltaic devices.

The team combined zinc bromide with methylammonium chloride to form a volatile eutectic mixture. A eutectic combination has a melting point lower than the relevant individual ingredients under suitable conditions. During heating, localized melting at perovskite grain boundaries helps crystals reorganize. X-ray nanoprobe measurements at Brookhaven's National Synchrotron Light Source II tracked changes in elemental distribution, including zinc enrichment near boundaries and a more uniform halide composition.

The efficiency and durability measurements

According to the October 8 Science paper, a top laboratory cell reached 26.5% measured efficiency, while independent certification established 25.9%. A larger minimodule exceeded 15 square centimeters and achieved 23.9%. The distinction between a research cell and a minimodule matters: efficiency can decline when many small devices must be connected, enlarged and manufactured consistently.

Researchers also tested operating stability. One small-area device started at 24.1% conversion efficiency and retained 85.8% of its initial performance after 1,400 hours at 85°C under continuous operation. Multiplying 24.1% by 0.858 gives approximately 20.7% retained conversion efficiency under the reported test. The measurement is significant, but a roughly 58-day laboratory test does not establish a 20-year or 30-year field lifetime under outdoor weathering.

Princeton researchers reported charge carriers remaining in the treated material for up to 300 microseconds, and described a roughly tenfold improvement in energy retention relative to untreated samples. Longer-lived charge carriers can reduce losses, but carrier lifetime should not be confused with battery-style energy storage. The authors suggested a possible additional 1% gain in electricity generation for leading cells, which remains a projection rather than a measured improvement across commercial solar panels.

The manufacturing question remains

Perovskite layers may one day sit on silicon cells in tandem modules, absorbing light that a silicon-only device uses less efficiently. BitcoinVersus.Tech previously covered Heliene and Caelux's tandem solar manufacturing plans. Unlike that industrial initiative, the new crystallization work reports a materials process in laboratory cells and minimodules, without a demonstrated mass-production yield or factory cost.

The result also fits broader research into solar-cell surface and thin-film characterization and alternative approaches such as organic semiconductor solar-fuel systems. Electricity storage and grid integration remain separate problems, as illustrated by recent battery-storage deployment data.

The central opportunity is improved control over crystal defects without abandoning solution-based fabrication. Remaining questions include large-area uniformity, chemical compatibility, long-term moisture and heat resistance, and repeatable yields at commercial scale. More durable and efficient solar cells could follow, but the next step is independent replication and field-relevant module testing.
