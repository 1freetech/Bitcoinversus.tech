Enel has agreed to buy 317 MWdc of solar projects in South Carolina from energyRe, adding another substantial portfolio to its North American renewable-energy strategy. The companies announced the transaction on November 20, 2026. Enel expects the plants to produce more than 540 gigawatt-hours of electricity annually once completed and operating. The acquisition has not closed, and the output remains a forecast, not delivered generation.

energyRe will continue managing construction through commercial operation. Enel expects the transaction to close during the first quarter of 2027, subject to standard closing conditions and required U.S. antitrust and foreign-investment approvals. Neither company has announced that all 317 MWdc are currently generating electricity.

What 317 MWdc and 540 GWh actually mean

The 317 MWdc figure describes solar panel nameplate capacity measured on the direct-current side. Grid delivery passes through inverters and is generally expressed in alternating-current terms, so 317 MWdc must not be mistaken for guaranteed power delivered at every moment. Sunlight, weather, module temperature, outages, site design and interconnection constraints determine real generation.

More than 540 GWh per year translates to over 540 million kilowatt-hours annually, equivalent to more than 1.48 GWh per day averaged over a year. The stated minimum corresponds to approximately 61.6 MW of average energy output; it is not an additional installed capacity rating. Actual results will be measurable only after construction and operation.

The deal includes financial forecasts, not realized earnings

Enel estimates an approximately $255 million increase in group net financial debt associated with the acquisition after completion and startup effects. It expects the plants to add roughly $30 million to annual EBITDA once operational. Both figures are management forecasts. The $255 million debt impact should not be described as the disclosed purchase price, and EBITDA is not net cash profit.

The Italian utility says its North American wind, solar and storage portfolio will reach approximately 13.5 GW after the transaction closes. Storage capacity is included in the reported portfolio metric, but a battery is an energy-storage resource rather than a primary source of electricity.

A third major U.S. solar transaction in 2026

On September 10, Enel completed a separate 810 MW U.S. solar portfolio acquisition with about 1.6 TWh of expected annual production. On October 2, it completed the purchase of approximately 270 MW of operational U.S. solar plants, expected to generate about 400 GWh annually. The new South Carolina agreement involves different projects, is not yet completed and carries a greater than 540 GWh forecast.

Why new generation still needs grid infrastructure

Even fully financed solar projects depend on transformers, substations, transmission access, grid studies and local construction schedules. A solar installation that finishes its panels before interconnection can still wait to export its full output. Recent coverage of a proposed 1.8 GW transmission connection illustrates the separate infrastructure required to move generation across regions.

Battery deployment can complement daylight generation, although the new deal does not specify storage included with the 317 MWdc portfolio. Sunrun’s reported battery expansion and the EIA’s electricity demand outlook provide wider context for balancing generation and demand. They do not establish the output or revenues of energyRe’s individual sites.

The next milestones are regulatory clearance, deal closing, completed construction and independently reported generation. Until those events occur, Enel’s agreement is a significant planned addition to its solar business, rather than a completed 317 MWdc increase in operating output.
