Scientists working with MicroQuanta Semiconductor and Zhejiang University have identified a major reason promising perovskite solar cells lose performance as they become larger modules: a declining fill factor. Their Nature Communications paper, published October 29, 2026, describes a cooling-assisted fabrication method that preserved electrical performance in a mini-module with 23.9% certified steady-state power-conversion efficiency over a 19.48-square-centimeter aperture.

The efficiency result itself is not a newly measured November record: MicroQuanta's 19.48 cm² certification was already represented in international solar efficiency tables covering 2025. The newer peer-reviewed contribution explains the manufacturing losses and shows how slowing crystal growth can limit them. PV Magazine reported the study on November 19, 2026.

Why a good solar cell can become a weaker module

A perovskite absorber converts incoming sunlight into electrical current. A laboratory cell can be only a few square centimeters, while a practical panel needs many connected subcells and substantially larger coated surfaces. As the area increases, grain nonuniformity, interconnections, inactive gaps and resistance can reduce the fraction of captured light converted into useful power.

The researchers analyzed open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current and fill factor, a measure of how closely a cell's current-voltage curve approaches the ideal maximum-power rectangle. Using Shapley-value attribution, a method for allocating contributions among several variables, they found that declining fill factor explained the largest portion of the scale-up efficiency loss after accounting for inactive laser-scribed areas.

Fifteen seconds of cooling changes crystallization

Immediately after depositing the wet precursor, the team cooled the film to 0–10°C for 15 seconds, then transferred it for vacuum treatment. Slower conversion into the finished perovskite produced larger, more uniform crystalline grains. The researchers used slot-die coating and laser scribing to build an eight-subcell series-connected mini-module.

The measured module combined 23.9% steady-state efficiency, an 83.3% fill factor and a 97% geometric fill factor, meaning nearly all of the aperture area remained active. The latter figure describes usable area, not electrical conversion efficiency. Under a separate continuous-illumination, open-circuit aging test, the fill-factor decline in cooled devices was less than one-third of the decline recorded in controls.

What the test does not establish

Mini-module performance cannot be substituted for the output of a full rooftop product. The tested aperture is only 19.48 cm², not 19.48 square meters. Longer outdoor lifetimes, uniform production across square-meter panels, encapsulation, moisture resistance, costs and eventual field yield require separate evidence.

Efficiency figures also require comparable test methods. A higher current-voltage scan result reported by MicroQuanta cannot simply replace the 23.9% independently certified steady-state value. The new paper does not prove a 25-year commercial lifespan or a full-scale panel operating at 23.9%.
