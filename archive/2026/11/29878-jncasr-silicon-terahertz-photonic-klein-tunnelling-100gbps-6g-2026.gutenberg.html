India’s Ministry of Science and Technology announced November 4, 2026, that researchers demonstrated up to 100 gigabits per second of terahertz data transmission through an engineered barrier on a silicon photonic chip. The experiment took place at the Jawaharlal Nehru Centre for Advanced Scientific Research (JNCASR), with collaborators in India, Singapore and France. The result concerns laboratory-scale guided terahertz signals, not a deployed 6G cellular network.

The peer-reviewed research, published October 11 in Advanced Materials, reports a silicon topological photonic crystal engineered with triangular holes. The geometry allows an electromagnetic signal to travel along a protected interface, cross a deliberately introduced obstacle and continue with comparatively little loss. The effect is known as photonic Klein tunnelling, referring to a wave analogue of a phenomenon in quantum physics.

A silicon lattice designed to carry terahertz signals

A photonic crystal is a material patterned to alter how electromagnetic waves propagate. Here, two regions of high-resistivity silicon contain a honeycomb-like arrangement of triangular holes. Their boundary creates a path for terahertz radiation. Adjusting the sizes of holes over a short region changes its effective barrier strength, letting researchers test whether the wave keeps propagating rather than reflecting or scattering away.

Measurements covered 310–360 gigahertz, corresponding to frequencies far above conventional cellular radio bands. A single engineered barrier added about 1–2% transmission loss, while two barriers introduced approximately 2–3% relative to the baseline, according to the government report. The results held within a particular range of engineered barrier strengths; the researchers did not demonstrate immunity to arbitrarily large obstacles.

Such robustness could eventually make compact terahertz routing components easier to design. Related topics appear in BitcoinVersus.Tech reporting on micrometer-scale silicon photonic bends and silicon photonics reliability requirements. Those projects concern different frequencies, applications and fabrication technologies.

Three devices, three different data rates

The researchers fabricated three configurations. A structure containing two barriers carried 48 Gbit/s, while an omega-shaped path with four sharp bends and a central barrier carried 60 Gbit/s. A separate power-splitter design transmitted data at 100 Gbit/s using 32-state quadrature amplitude modulation, or QAM-32, which encodes information through variations in carrier amplitude and phase.

The headline 100 Gbit/s figure therefore belongs to the splitter experiment; it is not the measured speed of every chip layout. At 100 gigabits per second, a theoretical 5-gigabyte file contains approximately 40 gigabits, corresponding to 0.4 seconds of idealized transfer time. Protocol overhead, coding, conversion and practical hardware would add time. The experiment also does not establish equivalent wireless throughput between phones or cellular towers.

Promising physics, unresolved engineering

The published scientific abstract reports that valley pseudospin was preserved by more than 98% across two cascaded barriers, helping quantify the intended topological behavior. The authors also identify a limit: transmission deteriorates when barrier strength approaches a characteristic coupling energy in the lattice. Their experiments demonstrate a controllable design principle, not a lossless circuit under every condition.

Future 6G systems might use high-frequency interconnects to route signals between chips, antennas or communication modules. Commercial use would still require compatible terahertz sources and receivers, packaging, efficient power consumption, temperature stability and repeatable fabrication yields. Related outdoor 6G field demonstrations and AI-native 6G network research address separate system-level problems; neither proves the JNCASR structure can be integrated into a complete radio.

The team led by Abhishek Kumar next proposes electrically, mechanically or thermally tuning the barriers, potentially creating dynamically reconfigurable routing paths. The immediate achievement is narrower but concrete: fabricated silicon devices guided terahertz data through selected obstacles at verified laboratory speeds. Independent replication, system integration and field measurements remain future milestones.
