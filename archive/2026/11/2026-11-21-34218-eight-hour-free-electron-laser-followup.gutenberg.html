The plasma accelerator behind Berkeley Lab’s earlier 1,000-fold optical-gain experiment later powered a free-electron laser for more than eight hours without an operator intervening. The achievement was published April 7, 2026, in Physical Review Accelerators and Beams, months before a November 12 Department of Energy spotlight revisited the earlier one-hour experiment. The longer run is an extension of the same research program, not a new November laboratory trial.

At Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s BELLA Center, scientists working with TAU Systems and university collaborators stabilized a roughly 100-terawatt drive laser that accelerates electrons through plasma. The paper reports approximately 100 MeV electron beams at one shot per second for over 10 hours, supporting more than eight hours of continuous free-electron-laser operation without operator input. The generated light remained at 420 nanometers, in the visible violet region, not at an X-ray wavelength.

How engineers stopped the laser drifting

The central challenge was not producing one exceptionally powerful pulse. Temperature shifts, air turbulence, vibrations and optical movement can shift a laser’s focus relative to the gas target. Tiny changes alter the accelerated electron bunch, which affects coherent light generation inside the undulator.

To control drift, the team tracked a lower-power, faster-repeating ghost beam alongside the main laser. A wavefront sensor measured the curvature linked to focal position; a motorized optical telescope corrected it through a feedback loop. A camera and motorized mirror also compensated for sideways movement. Berkeley Lab reports the system largely eliminated long-term longitudinal drift and reduced pointing-position jitter by 40%. The improvement refers to alignment fluctuations, not a 40% energy saving.

What eight hours does and does not prove

The earlier BitcoinVersus.Tech report covered the July 29, 2025 paper demonstrating greater than 1,000-fold optical gain and lasing on more than 90% of shots during a one-hour measurement. The April 2026 publication instead addresses sustained operation. An eight-hour run is a longer demonstrated operating window, but not a measured eightfold improvement in wall-plug efficiency, equipment lifetime or per-shot laser reliability.

A scientific light source must maintain alignment across a realistic experiment rather than reach peak output briefly. Nevertheless, the 2026 paper does not establish a finished X-ray user facility, commercial cost parity, or independent performance over years of service. Researchers still need to improve beam control, photon extraction and facility integration.

Next target: shorter wavelengths, higher energy

Berkeley Lab identifies free-electron-laser saturation and higher electron energy as objectives. The researchers discuss increasing beam energy toward 500 MeV and moving the output toward 20–30 nm. Both are future goals rather than results from the eight-hour test. Even the proposed 20–30 nm range is substantially shorter than 420 nm and would require new verification of stable lasing.

Compact photon sources could eventually assist materials studies and chip research, but relevant technologies are not interchangeable. Semiconductor fabrication requires the precision and uptime examined in our manufacturing-equipment review. Atomic-force microscopes measure surfaces by a different mechanism, while nanoscale lasers and integrated photonic chips pursue separate applications.

The verified advance is operational stability: a sensitive plasma-driven electron source and free-electron laser continued working for an extended unattended period. Lower equipment cost, compact X-ray operation and industrial deployment remain promising but unproven.
