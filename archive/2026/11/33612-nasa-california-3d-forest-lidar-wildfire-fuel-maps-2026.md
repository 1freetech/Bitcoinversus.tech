# NASA’s 3D Forest Maps Reveal California’s Hidden Wildfire Fuel

*Verbatim published Gutenberg markup, including the canonical volunteer footer and source-attributed multimedia.*

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>NASA has highlighted a new way to see California's wildfire danger: <strong>three-dimensional lidar maps</strong> that reveal tree canopies, vegetation density and the terrain beneath them. A <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/science-news/2026/11/18/california-wildfire-risk-3d-maps/">November 18, 2026 NASA science update</a> describes a statewide mapping effort by its Wildfire, Ecosystem Resilience, and Risk Assessment Initiative, or WERK. The data can help land managers identify where combustible vegetation links the ground to treetops.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The mapping initiative did not begin in November. California's first consistent statewide lidar-derived datasets were <a href="https://wildfiretaskforce.org/california-unveils-first-ever-statewide-lidar-maps/">released December 12, 2025</a>. NASA's latest coverage draws attention to the project's detailed point clouds and future wildfire-risk applications, rather than announcing a sensor that can detect every new fire in real time.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why a laser sees wildfire fuel that a photograph misses</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Airborne lidar sends repeated laser pulses toward the ground and measures their return times. Millions of distance samples create a <strong>3D point cloud</strong>, allowing scientists to estimate the bare ground, branches, shrubs and tree canopy separately. Overlapping vegetation forms so-called ladder fuels, which can help a surface fire climb into the crowns of taller trees.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>NASA used aircraft survey data from the U.S. Geological Survey's <a href="https://www.usgs.gov/3d-elevation-program">3D Elevation Program</a> and processed them through the NASA Earth Exchange. WERK says it has assembled more than <strong>100 million acres</strong> of California lidar records into consistent derived products. A scan's collection year matters: a tree removed after the flight can still appear in an older map.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">One-meter detail is not the same as statewide one-meter coverage</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>NASA's short announcement describes one-square-meter pixels and tree-scale detail. Its more detailed <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/ames/earth-science/werk/">WERK project documentation</a>, however, identifies <strong>10-meter and 30-meter statewide layers</strong> and lists <strong>1-meter products for selected areas</strong>. Public catalogs include 1-meter datasets around the 2018 Camp Fire and 2025 Los Angeles fire zones. A blanket claim that every tree is already individually resolved in a uniformly available 1-meter statewide layer goes beyond the public documentation we verified.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Even at one-meter resolution, individual tree identification depends on overlapping crowns, survey density, seasonal leaves and processing methods. A <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/16/osrec-015-occupancy-grid-mapping-log-odds-lidar-navigation-costmaps/">BitcoinVersus.Tech lesson on lidar occupancy mapping</a> explains why measured returns need careful interpretation before becoming a useful map.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What fire planners can do with the maps</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>WERK aims to estimate vegetation height, shrub density, canopy structure, damaged buildings and changes after forest treatments. A local agency might prioritize work where dense undergrowth connects to tall trees near homes, then validate the site with field inspections. The approach complements emergency detection tools rather than replacing crews or predicting ignition dates.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:image {"id":33610,"sizeSlug":"large","linkDestination":"none"} --><figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img src="https://bitcoinversus.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/opentopography-california-dinkey-creek-forest-lidar.png?w=1024" alt="OpenTopography 3D lidar visualization of Dinkey Creek in California with multicolored tree canopy and terrain elevations" class="wp-image-33610" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">Archival 2010 Dinkey Creek forest scan, courtesy of OpenTopography and US Forest Service. <!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The state's next work includes individual-tree monitoring, land-cover classification and annual disturbance mapping. NASA's program page outlines phased releases through <strong>2028</strong>. The technical challenge is keeping the imagery current as trees die, regrow, burn or are cleared, while ensuring coverage and quality remain comparable across different aircraft surveys.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Similar evidence problems arise in other fields. Our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/19/oklahoma-earthquake-usgs-3079-events-hexbin-2013-2025/">USGS earthquake mapping analysis</a> separates measured events from a forecast of future hazards. <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/01/comcast-fiber-sensing-real-time-network-monitoring/">Distributed fiber sensing</a> and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/15/hqvt-multispectral-ai-data-center-fire-risk/">multispectral fire-warning systems</a> illustrate different technologies that monitor changing conditions rather than provide a historical terrain baseline.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Lidar reveals possible fuel pathways, but fire behavior still depends on weather, ignition and field conditions.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
