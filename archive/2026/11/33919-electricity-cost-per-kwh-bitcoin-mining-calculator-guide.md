# What Does Electricity Cost per kWh? A Bitcoin Mining Guide

*Verbatim published Gutenberg body. The canonical volunteer footer, original donation QR and repaired YouTube embed blocks are preserved.*

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>How much does it cost to run a Bitcoin miner, GPU workstation or data-center rack? Start with the price in <strong>dollars per kilowatt-hour ($/kWh)</strong>, the equipment's measured power and operating hours. A 3,500-watt ASIC operating continuously uses <strong>84 kWh daily</strong> or <strong>2,520 kWh over 30 days</strong>. At $0.10/kWh, its energy-only monthly cost is <strong>$252</strong>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The <a href="https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/measuring-electricity.php">U.S. Energy Information Administration</a> defines one kilowatt-hour as one kilowatt of power used for an hour. A kilowatt, abbreviated kW, measures power; kWh measures energy. The price unit is <strong>$/kWh</strong>, not “$/kW/h.” Rates differ across utilities and contracts.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The three-step electricity cost formula</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Step 1:</strong> Divide power in watts by 1,000. A 3,500 W miner uses 3.5 kW. <strong>Step 2:</strong> Multiply kW by operating hours. Continuous operation gives 3.5 × 24 = 84 kWh daily. <strong>Step 3:</strong> Multiply energy consumed by your rate. At $0.10/kWh, 84 × $0.10 = <strong>$8.40 daily</strong>; 30 days cost $252.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>For a full 365-day year, 3.5 × 8,760 = <strong>30,660 kWh</strong>, or $3,066 at ten cents per kWh. Actual monthly totals depend on runtime, machine settings, power fluctuations and downtime. Read energy from an appropriate meter instead of treating the manufacturer's typical specification as exact.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">An electricity tariff can charge more than energy</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>An advertised $0.06/kWh supply price may exclude delivery, fixed charges, taxes and demand costs. For many bills, dividing the relevant total by billed kWh provides an <strong>effective delivered rate</strong>, provided the included charges are stated. Commercial tariffs may assess peak demand separately in dollars per kW. The <a href="https://www.nlr.gov/reopt/curriculum/interpreting-utility-bills-rate-data">National Laboratory of the Rockies</a> explains the distinction.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Ten 3.5 kW miners require 35 kW and consume 25,200 kWh in a continuous 30-day month. At $0.10/kWh, energy costs $2,520. An illustrative <strong>$10/kW monthly demand fee</strong> adds another $350 if the applicable billed peak is 35 kW. Tariffs may instead use peak intervals, minimums or time-of-use periods. Never assume the same fees apply everywhere.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Include ventilation, cooling and real equipment conditions</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Fans, pumps, networking and transformer losses raise facility electricity use. If a miner consumes 2,520 kWh and auxiliary systems add an illustrative 10%, total energy rises to <strong>2,772 kWh</strong>, or $277.20 at $0.10/kWh. The 10% overhead is an example, not a standard mining-site measurement.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Mining profit requires a separate analysis of earned Bitcoin, hashprice, pool fees, network difficulty and hardware depreciation. Our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/01/bitcoin-antminer-s21-200ths-hashprice-electricity-breakeven-november-2026/">ASIC breakeven example</a> links hashprice to an affordable electricity rate. <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/11/19/texas-wind-power-collapse-bitcoin-mining-45-percent-uneconomic-hours-november-2026/">Texas price spikes</a> demonstrate why running every hour can be uneconomic.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The formula worth remembering</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours × dollars per kWh.</strong> Add relevant delivery, demand, fixed fees and cooling expenses for a full bill. Do not confuse a low energy-only quote with an all-in cost, or electricity expense with the profitability of mining itself.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
