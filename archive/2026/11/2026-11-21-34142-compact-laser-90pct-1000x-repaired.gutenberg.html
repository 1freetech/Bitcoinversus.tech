STATS | Science & Accelerator Engineering — A laser-plasma accelerator has powered a free-electron laser with optical gain exceeding 1,000 and successful lasing on more than 90% of shots during a one-hour test. The U.S. Department of Energy highlighted the findings on November 12, 2026, revisiting research originally published July 29, 2025 in Physical Review Letters. The distinction matters: the experiment is established research, not a newly completed November 2026 trial.

Why a smaller accelerator matters

Conventional high-energy accelerators rely on radio-frequency structures that can occupy large facilities. Laser-plasma accelerators instead send an intense laser pulse through plasma, creating an electric-field wake that accelerates electrons over a comparatively short distance. Producing energetic electrons is only part of the challenge. A free-electron laser also needs sufficiently consistent, high-quality electron bunches to amplify light as they pass through a magnetic undulator.

Researchers led by S. K. Barber at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, working with collaborators including Tau Systems, reported a free-electron laser operating at a wavelength of 420 nanometers, in the visible violet region. Its measured optical gain exceeded 1,000-fold in the self-amplified spontaneous-emission regime. The reported exponential gain-length bounds were 16.7–22.5 centimeters. These are laboratory performance measurements, not estimates of electricity savings or production cost.

Primary research: Barber et al., Physical Review Letters, July 29, 2025. Recent DOE context: DOE Basic Energy Sciences, November 12, 2026.

What the 90% figure measures

During an hour of operation at a stated repetition rate of 1 hertz—one shot per second—the team observed free-electron lasing on more than 90% of shots. Here, reliability is defined specifically as the percentage of shots displaying free-electron-laser gain. It does not mean 90% facility uptime, 90% wall-plug efficiency or 90% commercial production yield.

At a perfectly uninterrupted 1 Hz for 60 minutes, the nominal opportunity count would be 1 × 60 × 60 = 3,600 shots. More than 90% of that hypothetical count would exceed 3,240 successful shots. However, the paper abstract does not establish that exactly 3,600 shots were recorded, so 3,240 must not be reported as the measured success count. The defensible reported observation is greater than 90% during the one-hour run.

STATS: Reliability versus optical amplification

What remains unproven

High gain and repeatable lasing address important obstacles for plasma-driven free-electron lasers, but they do not establish a complete, compact X-ray laser ready for commercial operation. The reported 420 nm output is visible light, not an X-ray wavelength. The same research group subsequently demonstrated over eight hours of unattended free-electron lasing in research published April 7, 2026; the later operating-duration result does not imply commercial X-ray output. Long-duration operational stability, beam control, system integration, operating cost and practical user-facility performance remain separate engineering questions.

The result is consequential because a reliable plasma-driven light source could eventually make advanced scientific instruments smaller and more accessible. That potential is a research direction, not a demonstrated reduction in total facility size, price or electricity consumption. Other emerging light sources include 115-nanometer research nanolasers and integrated light-emitting and amplifying chips, neither operating through the same electron-beam mechanism. For industrial context, semiconductor manufacturing systems and atomic-force metrology illustrate different performance demands.
