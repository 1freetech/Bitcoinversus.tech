A Seattle startup has tested a geothermal power plant nearly a mile beneath the Pacific Ocean—and generated more electricity than it consumed for just 8.7 seconds. Endurance Energy disclosed the result on October 19, 2026, after its September 27 demonstration at Axial Seamount, roughly 300 miles off Oregon. The test established a striking engineering milestone, but not sustained commercial generation.

A 16-meter well, but power from a natural vent

The company's three-ton Adélie prototype was lowered to the seabed and remotely commanded from Seattle through the National Science Foundation's fiber-optic Regional Cabled Array. Crews drilled a 16-meter well in the volcano's caldera, then installed casing and a valve. The well produced fluid at 260°C but could not supply enough flow to run the plant.

Instead, Adélie used a nearby natural hydrothermal vent at approximately 281°C. Heat from the fluid drove an organic Rankine-cycle turbine at about 26,000 rpm. Endurance reported 2 kW median net output and 16.5 kW peak net output during the short positive-power interval. Those figures measure net electrical power, not total electrical energy or the device's proposed 100 kW design rating.

Why the test stopped

The run ended after a battery overcurrent-protection trip. A failed power cable supplying the refrigerant pump stopped a subsequent restart attempt. IEEE Spectrum's October 27 update also describes water intrusion at a cable seal. The critical engineering lesson is reliability under corrosive, high-pressure ocean conditions.

This differs from Fervo's operating 33 MW geothermal block in Utah, which produces contracted grid electricity on land. Endurance's prototype has not demonstrated useful continuous output. Its remote communications depend on the same kind of infrastructure examined in our subsea cable repair reporting.

What happens next

The location itself matters. USGS says Axial Seamount is a highly active underwater volcano. The university-operated observatory already studies its vents and ecology. Endurance says it mapped local habitats, sampled water and collected environmental data; longer-term impacts still require assessment. The effort also complements wider research into offshore energy infrastructure.

Endurance plans a rebuilt Gentoo prototype this winter and a proposed 2 MW Leopard generator, with a first commercial project targeting Tonga in 2027. The firm has not proved those future targets. Heat-exchanger fundamentals explain the underlying physics, but cables, flow rates, marine maintenance and cost will decide whether the concept works beyond the laboratory.
