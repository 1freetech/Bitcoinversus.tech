Valve has opened direct purchases of its 512GB Steam Machine, but the larger 2TB version faces a manufacturing bottleneck as memory and storage prices rise. In a November 19, 2026 Steam Hardware update, the company said it had cleared every 512GB reservation and sent purchase invitations to the remaining 2TB reservation holders. A regular checkout is a meaningful change for the lower-capacity model, although availability remains limited to existing inventory.

The two models now have different paths

The 512GB model, originally priced at $1,049 in the United States, can be purchased without joining a new queue while stock remains. Valve says that if the current inventory sells out, its storefront will switch to an out-of-stock notice rather than open another waiting list. The 2TB version, priced at $1,349, has stopped accepting new waitlist registrations. Existing customers keep their places, but Valve does not guarantee that every person waiting will receive a unit. The Verge's November 19 report independently describes the change.

The basic price gap is $300, or approximately 28.6% of the smaller model's price. In return, the nominal SSD capacity rises fourfold, from 512GB to 2TB. Yet the difference cannot be interpreted as the cost of a standalone SSD upgrade: both prices include the complete gaming computer, and Valve has not published its component purchasing costs.

Why memory prices matter

Valve attributes the constraints to difficulty sourcing components, particularly memory and storage. The company says future production quantities will be smaller than planned and that it cannot yet specify future prices. The company expects to share more information in January 2027, but that is an update window, not a promised restock date.

According to Valve's published specifications, the cube-shaped PC uses a six-core, twelve-thread AMD Zen 4 processor, a 28-compute-unit RDNA 3 graphics processor, 16GB of DDR5 system RAM and 8GB of GDDR6 graphics memory. The two memory pools serve different functions; adding them together does not create 24GB of interchangeable system RAM. Each unit also requires an NVMe SSD, exposing Valve to both DRAM and flash-storage supply pressures.

The hardware runs SteamOS 3, an Arch Linux-based operating system, and supports a controller-oriented interface. Unlike a locked console library, Valve positions the machine as a compact PC for Steam games and streaming. BitcoinVersus.Tech has also covered Xbox's PC-game strategy and Linux's rising share in Steam's hardware survey, both relevant to competition beyond conventional consoles.

A choice for buyers, not a guaranteed reservation

Valve offers existing 2TB waitlist customers another option: purchase a 512GB system, provided stock remains, and surrender their original position. Whether that trade makes sense depends on a buyer's storage needs, expected game installation sizes and tolerance for later upgrades. A look at gaming storage history illustrates how dramatically capacity needs have expanded, while cloud gaming alternatives offer a different approach to accessing PC games.

The broader signal is economic rather than a newly announced processor limitation. Valve has completed an initial reservation phase, but component availability now determines how many additional boxes can reach customers. Higher costs may affect a future production run; no confirmed new price, sales total or production target has been announced. Ars Technica's reporting likewise distinguishes possible price adjustments from an announced increase.
