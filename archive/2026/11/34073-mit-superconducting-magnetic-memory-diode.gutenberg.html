MIT scientists have demonstrated an ultrathin superconducting memory cell that also controls the direction of electrical current. Announced in an MIT research report on November 20, 2026, the device stacks a superconducting film between two magnetic insulating layers. Its goal is to reduce the memory and signal-routing bottlenecks facing future low-temperature computers.

The peer-reviewed results appeared in Physical Review Letters on October 5, 2026. Researchers demonstrated selective switching of individual memory cells and a programmable superconducting diode. Neither a mass-produced memory chip nor a full computer with independently measured energy savings has been demonstrated.

How three layers remember one bit

Superconductors carry electrical current without ordinary resistance below a sufficiently low critical temperature. The new memory sandwiches an ultrathin superconducting layer between two ferromagnetic insulators. The relative direction of the two magnetic layers determines whether the middle film remains superconducting or becomes resistive at the operating temperature.

When the magnetizations point in opposite directions, the cell can conduct in its superconducting state. When they align, the superconducting state is suppressed and resistance appears. Those two distinct conditions encode a digital 0 or 1. A small magnetic field and a brief electrical pulse heat a selected cell enough to change its magnetic alignment.

Once written, the magnetic configuration persists without continuous writing power. That makes the memory nonvolatile while maintained under cryogenic operating conditions. Researchers also switched one cell without erasing a neighboring cell, a necessary step toward individually addressable memory arrays.

One device can steer electrical signals too

At a lower operating temperature, the same layered architecture becomes a programmable superconducting diode. It can favor nearly resistance-free current in one direction, switch the favored direction, or block transmission by entering a resistive state. The research paper reports zero-applied-field diode efficiencies exceeding approximately 60% in magnitude.

The percentage is a measure of current-direction asymmetry under the experiment’s conditions. It is not a claim that a completed computer consumes 60% less electricity. By combining memory and current control in one small structure, engineers could eventually reduce the need for separate components inside specialized cryogenic circuits.

Could superconducting memory reduce AI computing energy?

Conventional processors waste energy moving data between logic, memory and storage, while large data centers must remove heat from thousands of chips. Superconducting electronics could reduce resistive losses in particular circuits, but extremely cold equipment also requires refrigeration, wiring and power-hungry supporting hardware. Total system electricity matters more than an isolated device’s impressive properties.

The MIT demonstration stores ordinary bits, not quantum bits. Its potential applications include superconducting processors, signal-control hardware near quantum computers and specialized neuromorphic systems. Scaling the cells into dense, reliable arrays, measuring write energy and including cryogenic overhead remain open engineering problems.
