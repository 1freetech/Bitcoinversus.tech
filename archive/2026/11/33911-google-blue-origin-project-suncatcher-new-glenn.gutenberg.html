Google has selected Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket for a dedicated launch of experimental AI-computing satellites, with liftoff targeted for the first quarter of 2029. The companies announced the agreement on November 19, 2026. Each Project Suncatcher spacecraft is planned to carry Google Tensor Processing Units with processing capacity roughly comparable to a rack of servers in a conventional data center.

The new agreement is a launch contract, not a completed mission or proof that large orbital data centers are economical. Google has not disclosed the contract price, an exact launch date, the number of satellites or their final mass. The plan is to place the computing spacecraft in sun-synchronous orbit, where solar panels may receive sunlight for much of each orbit.

Why New Glenn matters for Google's space computing plan

Unlike an ordinary rideshare launch, a dedicated heavy-lift mission gives Google a larger payload opportunity for multiple computing spacecraft. Blue Origin's official announcement identifies the mission as the first dedicated heavy-lift launch for the Suncatcher research program. It does not promise a commercial orbital cloud service in 2029.

The satellites would be connected by high-speed optical links, potentially allowing workloads to run across several separate processing units. Google's earlier research describes laser communication between spacecraft, radiation testing of its chips and the thermal engineering needed to move processor heat into the vacuum of space.

A terrestrial data center can use fans, liquid cooling and water-based heat rejection. Orbit provides no air for convection, so engineers need heat pipes, large radiating surfaces and careful electrical power management. Long-duration radiation exposure, component replacement and the expense of launching hardware remain major unresolved constraints.

Google has already run AI in orbit

Project Suncatcher's first prototype launched on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rideshare mission on October 1, 2026, built in collaboration with Planet. By October 30, Google reported that its onboard TPU had run the Gemma 4 model and sent back a generated haiku during a ground-station pass. That demonstration showed a small real AI workload executing in orbit; it did not demonstrate an interconnected fleet or continuous operation of a data center.

What must happen before 2029

Google's next announced experiment involves two interconnected satellites in 2027, intended to test the precision and data rates of laser links between moving spacecraft. Large-scale AI computing would also require sustained electrical power, fault-tolerant networking and the ability to dissipate heat despite repeated heating and cooling cycles.

The company describes Project Suncatcher as a long-term research effort, not a replacement for existing Earth-based data centers. Orbital solar exposure might eventually reduce certain terrestrial electricity demands, but manufacturing, launches, communications, maintenance and heat rejection all carry their own costs and environmental trade-offs.

For background, the publication also covered NASA's addition of a larger New Glenn variant to its launch catalog and Google's original orbital TPU proposal. The newest milestone is a specific launch-provider commitment, with a date target three years away rather than an operational cloud platform.

The important result is straightforward: Google has secured a heavy-lift route for its next stage of orbital AI research. The unanswered question is whether reliable, economically competitive computing can follow the hardware into orbit.
