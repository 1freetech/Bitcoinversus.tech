Bitcoin mines could face new electrical-grid oversight. On November 16, 2026, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) asked federal regulators to approve three reliability standards for qualifying large computational loads, including cryptocurrency mines and AI data centers. The filing covers grid planning, operator communications and disturbance monitoring. The proposals are not yet enforceable federal rules.

Why miners can affect electrical reliability

Thousands of ASIC power supplies can draw megawatts, then change demand rapidly. Voluntary curtailment during expensive electricity hours can help a mining business. An unexpected mass shutdown during a voltage disturbance presents a different problem: electricity generation temporarily exceeds the remaining load and grid operators must rebalance.

NERC reviewed 26 major cryptocurrency-mining load-reduction events in Texas from January 2023 through September 2025. Selected events involved losses ranging from 17% to 95% of pre-disturbance consumption. The percentages describe observed events, not failure rates for every mining site.

The three proposed standards

The American Public Power Association's November 17 report identifies CLO-001-1, CLO-002-1 and CLO-003-1. The proposals also affect utilities, transmission planners and balancing authorities, helping grid operators obtain accurate models, timely operational information and disturbance evidence.

The proposed scope is computational-load sites with at least 50 MW of connected demand supplied through equipment connected at 100 kV or higher. Ordinary home miners are not automatically covered. Facility-specific obligations depend on the adopted definitions, electrical configuration and future regulatory approval.

What ride-through really means

Grid operators also want computational loads to tolerate routine voltage and frequency disturbances, a capability known as ride-through. The November foundational standards primarily establish planning, data, communications and protection-monitoring duties. Further performance requirements remain under separate development and regional work.

PJM described nearly 4,000 MW of data center demand disconnecting during a July 22 disturbance in northern Virginia. The facilities were not identified as Bitcoin mines. The incident illustrates why sudden demand loss, rather than electricity consumption alone, worries transmission operators.

What mining engineers should watch

Large operators may need improved model validation, fault recording, protection-setting coordination and reliable communication with system operators. Requirements may increase engineering and compliance costs, but could improve shared understanding of large electronic loads.
