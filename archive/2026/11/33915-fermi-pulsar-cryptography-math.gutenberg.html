A team of astrophysicists has uncovered a rapidly spinning neutron star hidden in 15 years of NASA Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope data. Their new mathematical approach borrows techniques from cryptography to search for gamma-ray pulsars with roughly one-millionth of the computation required by earlier methods, according to research published on November 19, 2026, in Physical Review Letters.

The discovery, named PSR J1622−7204, rotates 261 times per second. Scientists identified its periodic signal using approximately 10 CPU-hours, even though an earlier supercomputer-assisted search failed after consuming the equivalent of around 600 single-core computing years. Researchers from the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics, the Weizmann Institute of Science and Chalmers University collaborated on the work.

How a hidden pulsar becomes a mathematics problem

Pulsars are the dense, rapidly rotating remnants of massive stars. Their magnetic fields accelerate charged particles, creating beams of radiation that periodically sweep past Earth. NASA’s Fermi telescope measures high-energy photons arriving from many directions, but an individual pulsar may complete 100 million rotations between two detected photons.

A conventional search tries many combinations of a star’s sky position, spin frequency and gradual slowdown, correcting photon timing for the motion of Earth and the telescope. Sparse photon detections create a vast search space. The new method turns missing whole-number rotation counts into points on a high-dimensional mathematical lattice, then seeks a configuration that makes the observed timing consistent.

Algorithms developed for cryptographic mathematics help reject immense numbers of incorrect combinations efficiently. The astrophysics application does not involve cracking passwords, breaking Bitcoin encryption or operating a quantum computer. The underlying optimization ideas have instead been adapted to reveal a periodic astrophysical signal.

What researchers actually measured

Scientists began with short trials using groups of roughly 80 candidate photons. Each trial took about five minutes on one CPU core; one of 20 trials yielded the correct candidate. Follow-up analysis of the fuller dataset confirmed a signal assembled from approximately 16,000 Fermi-detected photons across about 15 years.

A previous, computationally expensive search had not established the pulsar. Consequently, comparing its failed 600-core-year run directly with the successful new search is not a controlled performance benchmark. The authors’ millionfold efficiency claim describes their improved search approach; it should not be interpreted as a universal speedup for every telescope analysis or scientific workload.

Why faster searches matter

Fermi surveys the entire sky approximately every three hours, accumulating vast public datasets that may contain many signals yet to be identified. Efficient processing lets research teams revisit previously unsuccessful searches without allocating centuries of processor time. The result also highlights why algorithmic improvements can sometimes unlock science more effectively than buying faster hardware.

Researchers hope to extend the approach to pulsars in binary systems, where orbital motion adds further complexity. Accurate pulsar timing can help test relativity and potentially support deep-space navigation, but neither application has been demonstrated by the new discovery alone.
