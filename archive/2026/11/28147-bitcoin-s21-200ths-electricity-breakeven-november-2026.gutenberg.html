Bitcoin mining economics tightened into November 1, 2026, as estimated hashprice fell to about $33 per petahash per second per day, down from $36.20 on October 24. For the original Bitmain Antminer S21 rated at 200 TH/s and 3,500 W, those figures imply $6.60 in daily gross mining revenue and an electricity-only break-even price of approximately 7.86 cents per kilowatt-hour. This is a calculation, not a guaranteed payout.

Bitcoin’s difficulty reached 148.36 trillion on October 29, a 5.54% increase confirmed by blockchain records. The resulting competition helps explain why revenue per unit of hashrate matters more than total computing power alone. The November 1 hashprice reading is a provisional daily estimate that can change with Bitcoin prices, transaction fees and block production.

What one 200 TH/s machine earns

The S21’s 200 TH/s equals 0.2 PH/s, so 0.2 times $33 is $6.60 a day before expenses. Bitmain specifies 3,500 W at 25°C, equivalent to 84 kWh over 24 hours at continuous rated operation. At five cents per kWh, electricity consumes $4.20 and leaves $2.40 before other expenses. At eight cents, power costs $6.72, leaving a 12-cent daily shortfall even before cooling, hosting, maintenance, purchase cost and taxes.

Why wall power matters more than advertised speed

The threshold is not a universal profitability point. Pool fees and downtime reduce realized revenue; cooling and power-distribution losses can raise effective energy costs. Actual hashrate and wall draw fluctuate, and a hosted miner may pay contract fees exceeding the power tariff. The distinction between nameplate and wall joules per terahash matters when comparing ASICs with different efficiency ratings.

For operators, the immediate decision is whether measured wall-power cost and accepted shares support keeping individual machines online. The next difficulty adjustment and Bitcoin’s market price will change these numbers.
