Goldman Sachs has restricted most bankers and traders from using advanced AI coding tools, including Anthropic's Claude Code and coding products from Microsoft's GitHub, Bloomberg reported November 2, 2026. Internal guidance revised in October generally reserves these tools for engineers. The bank has not banned all artificial intelligence.

Executive Summary's X post discussing reports of Goldman Sachs restrictions on employee AI coding tools.

Who lost access—and who did not?

Traders reportedly used coding agents to monitor client flows, model positions and prepare material. Some objected to losing access. Engineers retain the tools, while strats—staff bridging trading, quantitative research and software development—are exempt, according to the report. Goldman said it assessed productivity and risk before assigning tools to suitable roles.

Goldman's September announcement counted over 12,000 engineers, roughly one-quarter of its workforce. The distinction matters: agentic coding can create software and interact with systems; access controls, code review and sensitive-data protections remain essential. Our reporting on malicious Claude Code extensions illustrates a related security concern, not a confirmed cause of Goldman's decision.

The bank still develops and uses AI

Goldman's own Code of Business Conduct explicitly permits approved AI through company systems. Its GS AI Assistant was launched firmwide in June 2025. It has also worked with Anthropic on banking automation and launched a small-business AI initiative in October 2026.

Goldman Sachs CIO Marco Argenti discusses how AI may change workplace processes in the firm's official AI Exchanges interview.

The change follows a broader experiment in employee-built software. In July, CIO Marco Argenti praised non-engineers prototyping with AI before handing work to engineering teams. Goldman now appears to favor tighter ownership of coding agents while keeping approved assistants accessible. Explore our GitHub AI coding-tools coverage for the technical background.

What happens next

The October directive's detailed internal wording has not been published publicly. In April, Goldman separately limited Claude access in Hong Kong, illustrating how restrictions can vary by location and role. The unresolved question is whether tighter controls preserve the productivity of AI prototyping without exposing client information or trading systems to additional risk.
