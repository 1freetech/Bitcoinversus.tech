U.S. oil refineries are producing a smaller share of gasoline and shifting their product mix toward diesel-range fuels and aviation kerosene. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported November 5, 2026 that gasoline yields averaged below 45% from January through August, below the previous five-year low in every month. High profit margins for diesel and jet fuel are changing refinery incentives. The latest monthly dataset ends in August; the release is not a measurement of November production.

A refinery yield measures output of a named petroleum product relative to crude-oil input. Historically, U.S. refiners have produced roughly 45%–50% finished gasoline. EIA's August data put finished gasoline yield at 44.1%, distillate fuel oil at 29.8%, and kerosene-type jet fuel at 12.0%. Yield percentages describe the composition of output, not a promise that total U.S. gasoline barrels or gasoline demand decreased by the same percentage.

Why refineries produce different fuels

Diesel, heating oil and jet fuel are known as middle distillates because their boiling ranges lie between lighter gasoline fractions and heavier refinery streams. A crude-oil furnace heats feedstock and a distillation tower separates hydrocarbon fractions by boiling characteristics. Refiners then use conversion units, hydrotreaters, blending equipment and catalyst systems to adjust finished-fuel volumes and specifications. The financial signal has been particularly strong in the distillate market, EIA says: yields of distillate fuel oil exceeded their five-year average in every month through August 2026. Diesel's applications include trucking, heavy equipment, heating and backup generation. Jet fuel reaches a new yield threshold

Jet fuel has shown another marked shift. Fleetwide kerosene-type jet-fuel yield exceeded 12% for the first time in March 2026 and stayed at or above 12% through August, according to EIA. April reached 12.5% in the monthly series. Aviation relies on concentrated liquid-fuel energy, a constraint discussed in BitcoinVersus.Tech's electric-aircraft engineering analysis. Higher jet-fuel refining yield, however, does not establish growth in passenger traffic or prove cheaper airline tickets.

Refiners cannot freely turn every barrel of crude into whichever product sells for the highest price. Conversion and hydrotreating capacity, feedstock chemistry, product specifications, hydrogen availability and maintenance schedules limit flexibility. EIA separately reported U.S. refinery utilization above 95% on average from May through September 2026, the highest comparable seasonal rate since 1998. High throughput and a changing yield mix are separate measurements: one tracks how much refining capacity is used; the other tracks the share emerging as each product.

Economic benefits and limits

The trend could support refinery margins and supply fuel to industrial and freight customers facing tight global inventories. Still, strong crack spreads do not guarantee sustained profit after crude procurement, energy, labor and capital costs. A push toward distillates can also tighten gasoline availability relative to demand, while higher export incentives may expose domestic buyers to global supply shocks. Refinery economics and consumer prices move together imperfectly.

Conventional petroleum diesel must also be distinguished from hydrotreated renewable diesel. The earlier BitcoinVersus.Tech report on renewable diesel in data-center backup generators examined a different fuel-production pathway; similarly named fuels should not be counted as identical crude-refinery outputs. Meanwhile, replacing combustion equipment with electric alternatives can lower some end-use fuel demand, as outlined in the site's electrification analysis. Such changes may alter future product margins without eliminating current needs.

Next evidence

The next useful evidence will be later Petroleum Supply Monthly revisions, the product-yield series through autumn, refinery utilization, inventories and export volumes. EIA's November 5 analysis shows a substantial change in the refinery product mix, not the end of gasoline production, a specified increase in absolute diesel output, or proof that one particular refinery reconfigured its equipment.
