A small geothermal microgrid planned for Pilgrim Hot Springs on Alaska's Seward Peninsula is moving toward construction in June 2027. KNOM reported on November 24, 2026 that the geothermal generator and battery system are being assembled away from the site. Unaatuq LLC and Kawerak are developing the installation to serve local facilities and reduce dependence on imported diesel. Unlike Fervo's utility-scale geothermal expansion in Utah, Pilgrim is designed as an isolated microgrid.

Converting low-temperature heat into electricity

The operator's renewable energy overview identifies an ElectraTherm organic Rankine cycle (ORC) generator rated at 75 kW, with approximately 35 kW of expected usable electricity. The geothermal water available for the initial installation is around 190°F (88°C). A heat exchanger transfers energy to a separate working fluid that vaporizes at suitable pressures. The vapor drives an expander coupled to a generator, then condenses and returns to the heat exchanger through a pump.

Low-temperature geothermal generation faces a smaller temperature difference between the heat source and cooling system than high-temperature projects. Available thermal flow, cooling conditions and auxiliary consumption affect usable electrical output. The technology differs substantially from the superhot geothermal approach proposed for Google's Oregon data centers.

The expected 35 kW is approximately 47% of the generator's maximum rating. At a constant 35 kW for 24 hours, the installation would supply 840 kWh. Neither calculation is a measurement of efficiency or actual daily production. Commissioning data will establish the operating output under local conditions.

An electrical grid without a utility connection

The battery energy storage system is intended to establish and stabilize the local electrical grid. Its power electronics must coordinate generation and consumption, including sudden load changes. A diesel generator will remain available as backup. The arrangement differs from Virginia's grid-connected substation battery, where storage operates alongside an established electricity network. A geothermal microgrid must also maintain voltage and frequency without relying on a large interconnected system.

The project's general manager described a $1.8 million federal grant supporting battery integration and related work in a March 2026 Department of Energy webinar. Equipment delivery depends on seasonal barge service and repairs to the access road from Nome. Commissioning is expected during summer 2027, with potential seasonal shutdown during the first winter.

A test of the larger geothermal resource

Earlier exploration indicates that a separate, deeper reservoir may support up to 5 MW, according to the researchers interviewed by KNOM. That estimate requires additional drilling and is not an approved expansion. Transmission economics would also determine whether a larger facility could serve Nome. The distinction is relevant to Indonesia's proposed 5 MW geothermal computing project, where electricity supply and customer demand must be established separately.

Pilgrim's initial installation is intended for community facilities, not Bitcoin mining or AI computing. Its value lies in demonstrating whether locally available heat can provide dependable electricity and reduce fuel deliveries in a remote environment. Measured output, diesel displacement, seasonal performance and reservoir response will determine the engineering lessons.
