Intel has signed its first external customer for the advanced 18A chipmaking family, a long-awaited milestone in its effort to manufacture chips for other companies. Chief executive Lip-Bu Tan announced the deal at the TomorrowX Summit in Austin, Texas, on November 18, 2026. Intel chief technology officer Pushkar Ranade subsequently clarified that the client selected 18A-P, the performance-enhanced version of the process. Intel has not disclosed the customer's name, contract value, chip design, expected wafer volume or delivery date. Data Center Dynamics and The Next Web reported the announcement on November 19.

Why one customer matters

Intel already fabricates processors for its own product divisions, including chips built on the original 18A technology. An outside customer is a different test of its foundry strategy. A competing chip designer must trust Intel with valuable intellectual property, commit engineering resources and accept the manufacturing process for an actual product.

The company has struggled to make external manufacturing a meaningful part of its business. Earlier BitcoinVersus.tech analysis found that outside clients accounted for only a small share of Intel Foundry revenue. A new signing is commercially encouraging, but it is not evidence that large-volume wafer shipments or a profitable foundry relationship have begun.

What makes 18A-P different?

18A combines RibbonFET transistors and PowerVia backside power delivery. RibbonFET surrounds nanosheet transistor channels with the gate, improving electrical control and leakage behavior. PowerVia routes power through structures behind the silicon, leaving more room for signal wiring above. Intel introduced both approaches as major steps beyond its older FinFET process technologies.

The P variant adds transistor, contact and interconnect refinements, along with design-technology co-optimization. According to Intel's published technical claims, 18A-P can offer over 9% more performance at comparable power or more than 18% lower power at comparable performance than 18A, under the company's evaluated conditions. Those are process-level research comparisons, not independent benchmarks of the undisclosed customer's future chip. Intel also says 18A-P retains 18A design-rule compatibility, helping customers reuse existing design infrastructure.

Signing is not the same as shipping

Intel said in June that 18A-P had entered risk production, an early phase used to validate fabrication and device behavior before sustained high-volume manufacturing. Signing a chip designer does not establish a successful tape-out, certified yields, reliable deliveries or meaningful revenue. Those milestones will determine the financial impact.

The company faces formidable competitors, especially Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., while pursuing future processes such as Intel 14A, already being offered to design partners through an early process design kit. The foundry model also requires an effective ecosystem of electronic design automation software, intellectual property blocks, packaging, testing and support.

The next questions for Intel

Observers should watch for a named customer, specific product tape-out, production timetable, manufacturing yield data and external foundry revenue. Without those facts, assigning the contract to Apple, NVIDIA or any other company would be speculation. Claims about broader foundry manufacturing capacity likewise need real output data; Intel's separate wafer-throughput improvement proposals are not evidence of delivered volume under the new deal.
