Automated attackers can target Bitcoin wallets within seconds when signing software repeats a secret number. University of Liège computer scientists Vincent Jacquot and Benoît Donnet identified 3,278 recoverable Bitcoin private keys through historical signature analysis. The finding received renewed attention in a November 20, 2026 CoinDesk report. The peer-reviewed study was published in March, not November, and does not identify a failure in Bitcoin's consensus rules.

One repeated number can expose a wallet

Legacy Bitcoin transactions often use the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm, or ECDSA. Every signature requires a temporary secret number called a nonce. Reusing a nonce to sign different messages with one private key can make that key mathematically recoverable from publicly visible signatures. Some patterns involving several keys can also leak secrets. The failure is in the signing implementation, not proof that correctly generated Bitcoin keys can be guessed.

The researchers scanned ECDSA signatures across Bitcoin, Ethereum, Dogecoin, Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash and Dash through June 30, 2024. Their separate-chain method recovered 3,605 distinct keys. Cross-chain analysis identified 15 additional keys, bringing the total to 3,620. The historical window matters: the total is not a live count of vulnerable wallets holding funds in November 2026.

What happened when researchers set a trap?

On April 28, 2025, the team funded intentionally vulnerable addresses with a few dollars each and monitored Bitcoin's mempool. Competing transactions appeared in the same second a repeated-nonce weakness became exploitable. Later transfers drained remaining bait. The researchers also observed attacks against Ethereum honeypots, with different timing and behavior. The experiment demonstrates active automated exploitation of a particular signing error, not that ordinary secure wallets are drained in seconds.

$106 million exposed is not $106 million stolen

The paper estimated that vulnerable addresses reached an aggregate €101 million, roughly $106 million, in historical peak balances using November 25, 2024 cryptocurrency prices. Those peaks occurred at different times. The authors could not establish how much money was actually stolen, and some withdrawals may have been legitimate. The estimate spans six blockchains, not Bitcoin alone.

Much of the historical exposure came from early Bitcoin activity. The researchers also found nonce reuse between chains, showing that signing mistakes can spread across ecosystems when implementations reuse weak signing material.

What can Bitcoin holders and developers do?

Developers should use reviewed signing libraries, safe nonce generation and established deterministic techniques such as RFC 6979. Tests should cover restored backups, hardware signers and edge-case application states. Users benefit from supported wallet software and trustworthy installers, rather than assuming every Bitcoin signature is unsafe.
