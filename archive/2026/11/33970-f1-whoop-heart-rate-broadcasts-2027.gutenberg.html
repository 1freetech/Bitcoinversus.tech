Formula 1 plans to put participating drivers’ live heart rates and selected physiological readings on race broadcasts beginning in 2027. The racing series and wearable manufacturer WHOOP announced a four-year partnership on November 18, 2026, expanding the familiar car telemetry story to include the bodies handling the speed, heat and physical strain.

WHOOP says it is the first consumer wearable approved by the FIA for use during Formula 1 races. Drivers may begin wearing approved devices at the Las Vegas Grand Prix weekend in November 2026. A full televised biometric presentation, called WHOOP Live, remains a 2027 plan, not an established live feature at the 2026 race. The Formula 1 announcement and WHOOP press release establish the timing.

How the broadcasts could work

Existing F1 graphics show speed, tire performance, lap times and racing gaps. WHOOP Live aims to add biological information during selected moments, such as the race start, close overtakes and the final lap. The parties say sharing will involve participating drivers, rather than every competitor automatically exposing a complete health record.

The scale remains unsettled. Formula 1 has not published a final list of participating drivers, individual broadcast fields, data-refresh rates or the exact design of the screen overlays. Reuters reported on November 19 that the expected group could reach double figures; the figure is an expectation, not a confirmed count.

How the wearable reads a heartbeat

WHOOP wearables use photoplethysmography (PPG). Tiny LEDs shine light into the skin while optical detectors record reflected-light variations caused by changing blood volume. Algorithms estimate pulse rate from the repeating signal. Accelerometers and other measurements help the software distinguish movement from physiological patterns, as explained in WHOOP’s technical description.

A racing cockpit introduces vibration, sweat, impacts, tight equipment and rapid movement. Motion can interfere with the optical signal, while fit and body placement influence measurement quality. The broadcast pipeline must also move readings from a wearable through communication systems to a graphics platform without distracting drivers or revealing more information than intended. The companies have not publicly quantified end-to-end latency or race-condition sensor error rates.

Will a high heart rate reveal who is stressed?

A pulse reading is informative, but it is not a direct meter of mental stress. Heat, dehydration, exertion, caffeine and physiological differences can all raise heart rate. A driver at 170 beats per minute may be working hard without experiencing the same psychological response as another driver showing an identical reading.

For broadcasters, the best application may be comparing one driver’s response across comparable moments, while clearly labeling sensor uncertainty and context. Our previous report on a USC optical stress-sensing ring explains how wearables infer physiological states. A measured pulse must not be presented as proof of fear or poor decision-making.

Consent, F1 Academy and the next data frontier

Sharing personal biometrics introduces a second challenge: who controls the data? Competing teams could draw their own interpretations from a rival’s apparent fatigue. Public broadcasts could also expose sensitive health patterns. Clear driver consent, restricted access, sensible retention limits and protections against misleading interpretations will matter as the partnership develops.

WHOOP will also partner with F1 Academy, the women’s development racing series. A separate Ferrari partnership announced in January 2026 already introduced the devices to that team, but a relationship with one team is different from an F1-wide broadcast program.

The expansion joins a growing sports technology movement. BitcoinVersus.Tech has reported on the FIA’s AI-assisted race-control center, NBA broadcasts with new AI camera views and FIFA’s wearable tracking standards. The genuine advance for F1 will come when verified, properly contextualized physiological data reaches viewers, rather than simply putting another number on screen.
