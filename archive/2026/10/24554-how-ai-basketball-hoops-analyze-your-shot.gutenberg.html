The basketball technology you were probably thinking of is Huupe. Tracy McGrady is now the company’s chief innovation officer and appeared with Vince Carter in a national campaign for its smart basketball systems.

Your technical guess was close, with one important correction: there is no clear public evidence that Huupe’s current systems rely on LiDAR. The company’s published material instead points to a mix of sensors, computer vision on some products, machine learning and software that turns raw shot data into feedback.

The first layer is sensing

A smart hoop has to convert a physical basketball shot into data. Huupe says its current systems can track makes, misses, shot location, trajectory, angle and other performance measurements. Earlier full-size designs used multiple cameras and computer vision, while the Huupe Mini and Arcade use sensor-based tracking without cameras. The company’s current Arena products also center on a smart sensor hub. Huupe’s own engineering history says an early rim sensor could not account for airballs, which pushed the team toward computer vision.

That basic approach is similar to other sports systems BitcoinVersus.Tech has covered, including IBM’s tennis biomechanics analysis, YOLO and OpenCV player tracking, and broader AI-assisted sports vision systems.

Then the software turns movement into meaning

Once the hardware captures a shot, the harder problem is interpretation. A Huupe patent describes image processing that performs line detection, adjusts captured imagery and feeds it into a trained machine-learning model to identify characteristics of a basketball action. Other Huupe patents combine sensor data, imaging data and a control unit to determine whether a shot was successful and generate recommendations. The imaging patent is strong evidence that the underlying stack is conventional computer vision plus machine learning rather than some mysterious new form of AI.

From there, software can estimate shot location, arc, entry angle, consistency, release characteristics and whether the ball touched the rim. Those measurements can be stored over hundreds or thousands of repetitions and turned into shot charts, trend lines and immediate feedback.

Does it learn from the best NBA shooters?

That part of the idea is plausible, but it should not be stated as fact. Huupe publicly discusses machine learning and elite-player involvement, but we did not find evidence that its models are specifically trained on a database of Stephen Curry, Ray Allen, Klay Thompson or other historically great shooters.

There is also a good reason not to define one universal “perfect” jumper. Elite shooters vary in release height, timing, footwork and arc. A useful system may be better at learning a player’s own repeatable mechanics, comparing good and bad repetitions, and identifying measurable changes than forcing everyone toward one NBA template.

The technology is real, but the AI is not magic

The underlying recipe is straightforward: sensors and/or cameras collect the movement, computer vision reconstructs the action, machine learning classifies patterns, and software returns feedback. The quality of the coaching still depends on calibration, clean data, model accuracy and whether the measured variables actually correlate with better shooting.

So the original guess was directionally right. The strongest public evidence points to sensor fusion, computer vision and machine learning. LiDAR does not appear necessary, and the claim that the system learns directly from the greatest NBA shooters remains unverified. The interesting part is that sports training is increasingly becoming the same engineering problem seen elsewhere in AI: measure the physical world, build a model of what happened, then turn the result into a decision a human can use.
