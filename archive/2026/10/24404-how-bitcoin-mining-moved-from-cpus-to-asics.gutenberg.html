Bitcoin mining began as a general-purpose computing task. In 2009, the software could use an ordinary computer’s CPU to search for valid SHA-256 hashes. There were few miners, network difficulty was tiny by modern standards, and specialized mining hardware did not yet exist.

GPUs changed the scale

By 2010, miners had begun moving work to graphics processors. GPUs could perform large numbers of simple calculations in parallel, making them far better suited to repeated hashing than a typical desktop CPU. That shift created the first real hardware arms race: more graphics cards meant more hashrate, but also more heat, power draw and increasingly elaborate home-built rigs.

FPGAs narrowed the job

The next step was the field-programmable gate array. Unlike a GPU, an FPGA could be configured around the hashing workload itself. The first open-source FPGA Bitcoin miner was released in May 2011, helping move mining away from general-purpose hardware and toward circuits optimized specifically for SHA-256.

ASICs ended the general-purpose era

ASICs—application-specific integrated circuits—removed most of the flexibility entirely. Their silicon was built to perform Bitcoin’s hashing workload at much higher throughput per watt. Canaan’s regulatory history says the Avalon team shipped its first batch of ASIC mining machines on January 19, 2013, marking a decisive commercial turning point. Canaan’s SEC filing identifies Avalon as the first batch of consumer Bitcoin mining machines incorporating ASIC technology.

Once ASICs arrived, mining economics changed permanently. CPU, GPU and FPGA miners could no longer compete on Bitcoin at the same energy cost. From that point forward, competition increasingly centered on chip design, power delivery, cooling, density and efficiency in joules per terahash.

The same race continues

The hardware changed, but the basic competition did not. Today’s miners chase lower energy per hash and higher machine-level hashrate. BitcoinVersus.Tech’s Bitcoin Mining Singularity research follows the efficiency side of that trend toward 1 J/TH, while its analysis of Landauer’s principle and mining efficiency examines the physical limits underneath it. The newest comparative analysis asks how long it could take before a single miner reaches 10 PH/s.

In less than four years, Bitcoin mining moved from software running on ordinary CPUs to silicon designed almost exclusively for one computation. Every generation since has been an extension of that same specialization.
