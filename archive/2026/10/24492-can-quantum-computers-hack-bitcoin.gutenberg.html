A quantum computer cannot hack Bitcoin today. The more useful question is whether a future fault-tolerant quantum computer could become powerful enough to recover Bitcoin private keys before the network has time to move to new cryptography.

In theory, the answer is yes. In practice, that machine does not exist yet, and Bitcoin’s quantum risk is narrower than the phrase “hack Bitcoin” suggests. The main threat is not that a quantum computer suddenly deletes the blockchain or turns off proof of work. It is that sufficiently advanced quantum hardware could attack the digital signatures that prove who is allowed to spend certain coins.

The biggest risk is Bitcoin’s signatures

Bitcoin uses the secp256k1 elliptic curve for ECDSA and Schnorr signatures. Those systems are secure against ordinary computers because recovering a private key from a public key requires solving an elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem that is computationally impractical at Bitcoin’s key sizes.

Quantum computing changes that assumption. Shor’s algorithm can, in principle, solve the mathematical problem behind elliptic-curve signatures much faster than classical algorithms. In March 2026, Google Quantum AI published new resource estimates for attacking the 256-bit elliptic-curve problem used by cryptocurrencies. Its researchers estimated that the computation could be performed with roughly 1,200–1,450 logical qubits, depending on the circuit design.

That does not mean a 1,200-qubit machine can steal Bitcoin tomorrow. Logical qubits require heavy error correction and can consume many physical qubits. NIST said in July 2026 that current quantum computers remain too small and unstable to threaten modern cryptography. The important change is that researchers are getting better at estimating the size of the future threat.

Not every Bitcoin output is exposed in the same way

The distinction between a Bitcoin address and a public key matters here. Some output types hide the public key behind a hash until the owner spends the coins. Others expose a public key directly on-chain. A future quantum attacker would have an easier target when a spendable output already reveals the public key needed for a Shor-style attack.

That makes old pay-to-public-key outputs and some modern constructions more directly relevant to long-exposure risk. Outputs that reveal their key only when they are spent have a different problem: if a future quantum computer became fast enough, an attacker could potentially try to recover the private key during the period between transaction broadcast and confirmation. None of those attacks are practical with today’s quantum hardware.

Quantum computing does not simply break Bitcoin mining

Bitcoin mining relies on SHA-256 hashing, not elliptic-curve signatures. The relevant quantum algorithm is Grover’s algorithm, which offers a quadratic search advantage rather than Shor’s much more dramatic attack on public-key cryptography.

That distinction is important. A quantum miner could theoretically search proof-of-work space more efficiently, but that is not the same as instantly taking over Bitcoin. Mining remains a competition for valid hashes, blocks still have to satisfy consensus rules, and network difficulty can adjust as available hashrate changes. Quantum hardware would have to compete economically and operationally with enormous fleets of specialized SHA-256 ASICs.

Bitcoin has time to migrate—but not forever

The Bitcoin development community is already debating possible defenses. BIP 360, currently a draft, proposes Pay-to-Merkle-Root outputs that remove Taproot’s key-path spend and reduce long-exposure risk. BIP 361, also a draft, describes a broader migration path that would eventually discourage and then restrict legacy ECDSA and Schnorr signatures after a post-quantum output type exists.

Neither proposal is an activated Bitcoin consensus change. They show that the problem is being treated as an engineering and migration issue rather than an unavoidable failure.

So, will quantum computing hack Bitcoin? Not with today’s machines. A future cryptographically relevant quantum computer could create a serious private-key theft problem if Bitcoin were still relying on vulnerable signatures when that day arrived. The network’s strongest defense is therefore not predicting the exact year quantum computers become dangerous. It is moving toward post-quantum cryptography before the threat becomes operational.
