Not today. Current quantum computers cannot break Bitcoin. But a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer could eventually threaten the elliptic-curve signatures that protect many Bitcoin outputs.

Bitcoin uses secp256k1 for ECDSA and Schnorr signatures. Google Quantum AI reported in March 2026 that optimized versions of Shor’s algorithm may require fewer resources than earlier estimates to solve the 256-bit elliptic-curve problem. That would matter most when a Bitcoin public key is already exposed. Google therefore recommends migrating blockchains toward post-quantum cryptography. Google Research’s paper summary says viable defenses exist, but deployment takes time.

The mining side is different. Bitcoin’s proof of work relies on SHA-256 hashing. Grover’s algorithm offers a quadratic speedup against hashes, not the dramatic key-recovery attack Shor’s algorithm gives against elliptic-curve cryptography. That means quantum computing does not simply make today’s ASIC mining hardware obsolete overnight.

Bitcoin developers are already discussing defenses. BIP 360 proposes Pay-to-Merkle-Root outputs that reduce long-exposure quantum risk, while broader post-quantum signature work continues.

The practical answer is simple: quantum computing is a future migration problem, not a present Bitcoin hack. The largest risk is waiting too long to upgrade wallets and consensus rules after cryptographically relevant quantum computers become plausible.
