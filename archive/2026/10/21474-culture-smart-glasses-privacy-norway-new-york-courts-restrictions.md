<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Camera-equipped smart glasses are moving from gadget novelty into a serious privacy fight. <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/countries-weigh-banning-smart-glasses-over-privacy-concerns-2026-10-06/"><strong>Reuters</strong></a> reported on October 6 that governments, courts, schools and companies are increasingly examining restrictions because smart glasses can photograph, film or record audio around people who may not realize they are being captured.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The tension is simple: smart glasses are useful precisely because cameras, microphones and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/04/meta-muse-gadgets-open-source-esp32-raspberry-pi-sdk/"><strong>wearable AI hardware</strong></a> disappear into something that looks much closer to ordinary eyewear. That same convenience makes traditional social cues around recording much weaker.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Norway Is Moving Toward a Temporary Ban</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Norway’s government said on October 5 that it plans to propose a temporary ban on AI glasses in selected public places while an expert group studies permanent rules. The official <a href="https://www.regjeringen.no/no/aktuelt/vil-innfore-midlertidig-forbud-mot-ki-briller/id3175516/"><strong>government announcement</strong></a> says possible locations include parks, beaches, museums, shopping centers, public events, schools and health facilities.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The proposal is not yet a blanket national ban. Norway still has to define exactly which devices and capabilities would be covered before legislation goes to parliament.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">New York Courts Already Draw a Hard Line</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>New York’s Unified Court System already prohibits smart glasses from court facilities. Its policy covers prescription and non-prescription eyewear or headwear containing cameras, microphones, computers or other technology capable of recording audio or video. The <a href="https://www.nycourts.gov/press/court-policy-prohibition-against-smart-glasses"><strong>court policy</strong></a> reflects a simple reality: recording hardware no longer has to look like a phone or conventional camera.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Meta Says the Recording Light Is the Social Signal</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/22/metas-muse-ai-agent-turns-personal-data-into-a-shopping-advantage/"><strong>Meta</strong></a>, whose Ray-Ban smart glasses helped push the category toward the mainstream, says capture LEDs activate when users take photos, record video or stream. Meta’s <a href="https://www.meta.com/actions/responsible-innovation/"><strong>privacy guidance</strong></a> also tells users to ask permission before capturing people and avoid sensitive locations where privacy is expected.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The policy debate is therefore moving beyond what the hardware can do to whether its notice mechanisms are strong enough. A tiny indicator light may be technically visible without being socially obvious to everyone nearby.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Privacy Is Bigger Than the Camera</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Smart glasses can combine cameras, microphones, voice assistants, cloud services and AI models in one always-available interface. That means the privacy question is not only whether an image was captured, but what information can be extracted from the scene after capture.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That mirrors a broader hardware-security lesson BitcoinVersus covered with a recent <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/06/crypto-hardware-10-dollar-open-source-wallet-self-custody-privacy/"><strong>hardware-wallet privacy prototype</strong></a>: protecting one layer of a device does not automatically protect the full information path.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Why This Is Becoming a Culture Problem</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Phones trained people to recognize when someone is obviously pointing a camera. Smart glasses weaken that convention because looking and recording can happen through the same wearable. That is why restrictions are appearing first in places where confidentiality, consent or vulnerability matter most.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Watch whether Norway’s proposal becomes law, whether other European countries copy device-specific restrictions, and whether manufacturers add stronger bystander signals or software controls.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
