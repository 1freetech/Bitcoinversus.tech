The White House has formally ordered executive-branch agencies to replace “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in federal usage, turning a terminology change into official policy while simultaneously unveiling a voluntary AI accord with major technology executives.

The move does not rename the field of artificial intelligence for universities, companies, standards bodies, or the public. It changes how the executive branch is instructed to refer to the technology and places the administration’s preferred language inside a broader push to frame advanced AI as a strategic national capability.

Executive Order 14434 makes the terminology change official

The September 29 executive order, titled Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence, directs federal agencies to use the new terminology as part of the administration’s technology policy.

The policy is mostly linguistic, but language inside government matters. Federal reports, agency websites, procurement documents, speeches, and policy discussions help establish how technologies are framed for regulators, contractors, lawmakers, and the public.

The White House promoted the change in an official X post declaring that America leads the future of Super Intelligence.

The same event also produced a voluntary AI accord

The terminology change was only one part of the September 29 event. Reuters reported that major technology executives also signed a voluntary accord covering AI standards and safety commitments.

Because the accord is voluntary, its impact depends heavily on how participating companies implement internal reviews, risk controls, and reporting practices. It does not function like a statute passed by Congress or a binding federal regulation issued through a rulemaking process.

The White House separately linked the signed order from another official X status.

The terminology is political branding, not a scientific redefinition

“Artificial intelligence” remains the established term across computer science, engineering, standards organizations, academia, and industry. The executive order cannot unilaterally redefine that global vocabulary.

What it can do is standardize how federal agencies talk about the technology. That makes the order closer to an official branding and policy-framing decision than a technical change to what AI systems are or how they work.

That distinction matters because “superintelligence” already has a separate meaning in AI research and public debate, usually referring to systems that substantially exceed human capabilities across many domains. Using “Super Intelligence” as a broad federal replacement for AI may therefore create tension between government terminology and existing technical usage.

AI governance is increasingly becoming a geopolitical issue

BitcoinVersus has already tracked U.S.-China discussions around an AI safety notification system, showing how advanced-model risks are becoming part of international diplomacy rather than remaining an internal technology-industry issue.

The policy debate is also expanding into questions of legal status and responsibility. The question of whether advanced robots could eventually receive legal rights shows how quickly technical capability can turn into a governance problem.

And governments are already intervening in algorithmic systems that affect everyday users. Australia’s push to give users more control over social-media algorithms illustrates the broader trend toward governments treating automated decision systems as a policy domain in their own right.

A name change can still influence procurement and regulation

Government terminology can travel. Federal contractors adapt to agency language. Policy documents are cited by state governments. Procurement frameworks influence vendors. Congressional hearings often borrow executive-branch vocabulary even when lawmakers disagree with the underlying policy.

That means the practical importance of “SI” may depend less on whether researchers adopt the phrase and more on whether it begins appearing in funding programs, contracts, security standards, export controls, and federal technology requirements.

The voluntary accord may matter more than the headline

The terminology change is the part most likely to attract attention, but the more consequential policy question may be the administration’s preference for voluntary commitments from major AI companies.

Supporters of voluntary standards argue that fast-moving technologies can outpace formal regulation and that companies need room to adapt safety practices quickly. Critics argue that self-policing can leave too much discretion with the same firms racing to deploy increasingly capable systems.

The September 29 package therefore combines two very different ideas: a mandatory terminology rule for federal agencies and a largely voluntary safety framework for the companies building the technology.

The White House has changed the government’s vocabulary; the industry still decides whether it sticks

Federal agencies now have clear direction about the administration’s preferred language. But researchers, standards organizations, software companies, and international partners are not required to follow it.

The result is an unusual split: inside the executive branch, “Super Intelligence” is official policy language. Outside it, “artificial intelligence” remains the dominant technical and commercial term. Whether SI becomes lasting vocabulary or a temporary political brand will depend on what happens after the executive order—not simply what the order calls it.
