<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Solo Satoshi introduced the <strong>Bitaxe Naja Duo 1201</strong> in August 2026, bringing two BM1373 ASICs into an open-source desktop Bitcoin miner. Its <a href="https://www.solosatoshi.com/product/bitaxe-naja-duo/">product specifications</a> list approximately <strong>4.4 TH/s at 42 watts</strong> in the default operating profile—equivalent to <strong>9.5 J/TH</strong> based on the vendor's numbers. That is an important silicon-generation step, but the figure is not the same as independently measured electricity at the AC outlet.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Key Takeaways</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Two Bitmain BM1373 ASICs drive a stock 4.4 TH/s target; Solo Satoshi calls the chips S23-class 3 nm silicon.</li><li>The seller advertises 42 W and 9.5 J/TH by its stated measurement boundary—not a verified wall-power result.</li><li>Previous independent testing discussed by BitcoinVersus.Tech found about 60 W at the outlet for 4.6 TH/s, or 13.0 J/TH.</li><li>The tested 8 TH/s overclock costs about 92 W in vendor-reported power and lowers stated efficiency to 11.5 J/TH.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Two BM1373 ASICs in a Desktop Miner</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The Naja Duo pairs a Thermalright AXP90-X47 cooler and 92 mm PWM fan with individual chip-temperature sensing, a two-phase voltage regulator, ESP32-S3 control, Wi-Fi, a 1.9-inch color display and <a href="https://github.com/bitaxeorg/ESP-Miner">ESP-Miner/AxeOS firmware</a>. The retail listing currently displays a <strong>$389.99–$409.99</strong> price range depending on configuration. Its 12 V input and XT30 connector distinguish the design from older single-chip Bitaxe models. For context, our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/24/bitaxe-gamma-603-154-ths-copperzilla-cooling-solomining/">Gamma 603 coverage</a> concerns a different, single-BM1370 model.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why 9.5 J/TH Is Not a Wall-Power Benchmark</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>At the seller's stated 327 MHz and 1,000 mV default settings, 42 W ÷ 4.4 TH/s is approximately <strong>9.55 J/TH</strong>. At a tested 600 MHz and 1,120 mV, the seller quotes 8 TH/s and 92 W—roughly <strong>11.5 J/TH</strong>. Both are vendor-reported values and the seller discloses ±15% variation. Power-supply losses and auxiliary consumption change the result at the wall.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/30/bitaxe-naja-duo-wall-tests-stock-efficiency-13-j-th/">September 30 Naja Duo wall-test report</a> reviewed independent data near <strong>4.6 TH/s at 60 W</strong>, producing approximately <strong>13.0 J/TH</strong> measured from the outlet. That is a different testing boundary, not a contradiction to a chip- or board-level rating. For a deeper comparison, read our <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/06/bitcoin-mining-hardware-nameplate-wall-facility-joules-per-terahash/">nameplate-versus-wall efficiency explainer</a>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Comes Next</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The Naja Duo illustrates how newer ASIC generations can bring more SHA-256 hashrate into a home-sized form factor. It does <em>not</em> prove a new network-wide efficiency rate or establish a date for the theoretical 1 J/TH milestone. Compare sustained hashrate, valid shares, temperatures, firmware version and measured wall power before incorporating any device into a broader power-efficiency trend.
