<div class="wp-block-group">
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>FIFA has opened three new technology challenges aimed at the next layer of football infrastructure: <strong>real-time player wearables</strong>, <strong>software-connected substitution boards</strong>, and <strong>fan systems</strong> that can improve the trip from buying a ticket to navigating a stadium.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The <a href="https://inside.fifa.com/innovation/news/building-future-game-three-new-challenges-launched-under-fifa-innovation-programme"><strong>FIFA Innovation Programme announcement</strong></a>, published September 29, says selected technologies could ultimately be tested and deployed at FIFA events. The three challenges focus on player health and performance, match operations, and the fan experience around the FIFA Women’s World Cup 2027 in Brazil.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Challenge One: Measure More Than Speed and Distance</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The wearables challenge is looking beyond conventional GPS-style tracking. FIFA says it wants systems that can measure new physical and physiological metrics in real time during training or matches, including <strong>hydration, load, recovery, and stress</strong>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That pushes sports wearables closer to medical and performance instrumentation. BitcoinVersus.Tech has already covered <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/01/sports-marshawn-lynchs-shoulder-pad-sensors-put-numbers-on-nfl-contact/"><strong>impact sensors in football equipment</strong></a>, where the hardware converts physical contact into measurable data. FIFA’s challenge asks for the same basic idea to move deeper into athlete physiology.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>FIFA requires proposed products to have reached at least the working-prototype stage. The technology also needs evidence that the sensors can capture data accurately and convert it into useful information rather than simply producing more numbers.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Challenge Two: Turn the Substitution Board Into a Networked Device</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The connected-substitution-board challenge is simpler to understand but technically important. FIFA wants lightweight, durable boards that can link directly to existing substitution-management software and automatically display the approved player numbers to the stadium audience.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That turns a traditionally standalone sideline device into part of the match-data network. Instead of an official manually entering numbers into an isolated board, software could move the substitution request through a digital workflow and push the same approved data to the display.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The direction is similar to other experiments in officiating hardware. The NBA has been testing <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/sports-nba-smart-basketball-referee-wrist-wearables/"><strong>Bluetooth-enabled basketballs and referee wrist wearables</strong></a>, showing how equipment that once operated independently can become another endpoint on a larger sports-data system.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">Challenge Three: Fix the Entire Fan Journey</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The third challenge focuses on the FIFA Women’s World Cup 2027. FIFA is asking for technology that can improve the entire fan journey—from discovering the tournament and buying tickets to transportation, accessibility, stadium navigation, in-venue engagement, and matchday operations.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The application window for this challenge is open until <strong>November 1, 2026</strong>. FIFA says selected products could be assessed and tested with a view toward deployment at the tournament in Brazil.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Independent analysis from <a href="https://www.marks-clerk.com/insights/latest-insights/102o463-what-fifa-sees-as-the-specific-technological-needs-of-the-game/"><strong>Marks &amp; Clerk</strong></a> highlights the same three priorities: end-to-end fan experience, real-time physiological wearables, and substitution hardware that can integrate with match-management software.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">The Bigger Trend Is Instrumented Football</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Football is gradually becoming a network of sensors, cameras, software services, and machine-readable events. Player movement can already be extracted from video, and BitcoinVersus.Tech previously covered how <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/02/sports-yolo-opencv-turn-football-video-into-live-player-tracking-and-heatmaps/"><strong>YOLO and OpenCV can turn football footage into live player tracking and heatmaps</strong></a>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The next step is connecting more of the physical game directly to the software layer. Wearables can expose physiology. Connected substitution boards can expose match operations. Fan applications can expose movement and demand around the stadium. Each system creates another structured data stream that teams, officials, operators, and broadcasters can use.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">The Hard Part Is Reliability</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Sports technology has a harsher requirement than ordinary consumer electronics: it must work while the match is happening. A sensor that loses connection, a substitution board that displays the wrong number, or a fan-navigation platform that fails under stadium traffic can become an operational problem immediately.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That is why FIFA’s language repeatedly emphasizes accurate data, working prototypes, durability, validation, and integration with existing systems. The technology is only useful when it can survive the speed, scale, and pressure of live football.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:ui-monospace,SFMono-Regular,Menlo,Monaco,Consolas,'Liberation Mono','Courier New',monospace">What to Watch Next</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The most important next step will be which vendors FIFA selects for testing. Those choices should reveal where the organization thinks the biggest technology gaps actually are: better physiological sensing, cleaner match-operations workflows, or a more connected stadium experience.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The direction is already clear. Football equipment is becoming connected hardware, stadium operations are becoming software workflows, and player performance is becoming increasingly measurable in real time.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
