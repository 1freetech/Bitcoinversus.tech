DTE Energy has brought its 132-megawatt Fish Creek Solar Park into operation near Sheridan, Michigan, adding a new source of renewable electricity to the utility's grid. The company announced the October 28 milestone for the $268 million development, which it says was financed through customers voluntarily enrolled in its MIGreenPower clean-energy subscription program.

The utility estimates that annual generation from the project could meet the electricity needs of about 30,000 homes. That figure is a household-equivalent estimate, not a claim that the installation can continuously provide 132 MW at night or during cloudy weather. PV Tech reported that construction was completed in August before the project’s operational announcement this month.

Customers Help Finance Michigan's New Solar Capacity

Under MIGreenPower, participating residents and businesses pay to support qualifying renewable generation and receive renewable-energy attribution toward their electricity use. DTE says this structure funded Fish Creek without adding its construction costs to rates for nonparticipants. It is different from the household rooftop economics described in our solar PV installation guide.

The company recently completed a separate 100 MW project, Cold Creek Solar Park, whose construction it announced finished on October 1. Similar investment is visible in Indiana’s recently approved solar and storage projects for a data center.

Local Grants and Long-Term Targets

Michigan environmental officials previously awarded $330,000 apiece to Bushnell and Evergreen townships for hosting the Fish Creek project. DTE says the development will also produce additional local tax revenue and payments to participating landowners, while having supported hundreds of construction jobs.

DTE reports that its Michigan renewable fleet now includes 37 solar parks and 20 wind parks, reflecting a wider expansion documented in the history of the solar energy industry. Its 2026 resource plan targets 15 additional gigawatts by 2042, not yet operating. The investment adds another operating asset to a broader debate over grid-scale storage and how utilities balance variable solar output with evening demand.

Future reports should show actual annual generation, operating costs and local tax receipts. For now, Fish Creek is operating, not just proposed.
