## Gutenberg source

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>OSHMS is a source-based historical certificate track investigating how human beings learned to select, transform and engineer materials.</strong> It begins before writing, using surviving archaeological evidence, and then includes named craftspeople and technical traditions preserved in scriptures, inscriptions, treatises, museum collections and later historical accounts. This is a learning and research curriculum, not a claim that its prehistoric makers can all be individually identified.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Scope of the Certificate</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Materials science is much broader than metallurgy. The chronological curriculum encompasses stone, wood, fibers, pigments, clay, kiln-fired ceramics, glass, native copper, bronze, iron, steel, cement, plastics, alloys, composites, electronic materials, atomic-scale fabrication and biomaterials. Technological craftspeople qualify for historical coverage even if their work predates the modern scientific discipline.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Earliest Documented Foundations</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>3.3 million years ago:</strong> evidence for stone-tool production at Lomekwi 3 in present-day Kenya, reported in <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14464">Nature</a>; the makers' names are unknown.</li><li><strong>c. 5000 BCE:</strong> early copper smelting documented at Belovode in Serbia, analyzed in the <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/inventing-metallurgy-in-western-eurasia-a-look-through-the-microscope-lens/F99DD8BDE33F603786CF81FB9871D316">Cambridge Archaeological Journal</a>.</li><li><strong>Named scriptural figure:</strong> <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/Genesis%204%3A22">Tubal-cain in Genesis 4:22</a>, described in the text as a metalworker or instructor of bronze and iron workers. His place in the textual tradition is certain; absolute date and independent personal identification are not.</li><li><strong>Named builders and materials:</strong> the <a href="https://quran.com/en/al-kahf/96-97">Qur'an, Al-Kahf 18:96–97</a> describes Dhul-Qarnayn's use of iron and molten copper to make a barrier. This is documentary evidence for a transmitted engineering narrative; the identity and date of the builder are separate historical questions.</li></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Historical Documentation Rule</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A maker can be included when a text names them, even when no securely attributed object survives. Include the text's historical and cultural setting and evidence limits. A scriptural passage supports the claim that a figure is <em>named in that source</em>; independent archaeological and historical confirmation of the person or action must be stated separately, not silently assumed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Record the person or culture, the exact original document (book/chapter/verse, inscription, manuscript, archive identifier) and the publication or artifact date.</li><li>Distinguish the date of a source from the date of the person or event it describes.</li><li>Document the reported invention, construction, experimental process or achievement; specify whether a named individual actually performed the work or is merely credited with it.</li><li>Accept scriptural and literary attestations as documentation of a named tradition without requiring artifacts, and explicitly state whether the identity or event has independent corroboration.</li><li>Record archaeological evidence and museum object numbers where available, but never make physical survival a prerequisite for inclusion.</li><li>Attribute alternative identifications, disputed dates and historical interpretations; distinguish legend, retrospective attribution and contemporary inscription.</li><li>Link primary material whenever available and cross-check translations or summaries with credible scholarship; revise entries when improved evidence appears.</li></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Course Sequence and Numbering</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>OSHMS.000–099:</strong> origins of tools and materials, early metallurgy, named craftsmen in written traditions, pottery, glass, iron and ancient material cultures.</li><li><strong>OSHMS.100–199:</strong> classical and medieval materials knowledge, technical crafts, steelmaking traditions and surviving manuscripts.</li><li><strong>OSHMS.200–299:</strong> early-modern treatises, experimental metallurgy, mineralogy and the scientific method.</li><li><strong>OSHMS.300–399:</strong> industrial metallurgy, mechanical testing, thermodynamics, phase diagrams and alloy development.</li><li><strong>OSHMS.400–499:</strong> polymers, ceramics, crystallography, semiconductors and twentieth-century materials science.</li><li><strong>OSHMS.500–999:</strong> advanced and contemporary materials: thin films, nanomaterials, quantum materials, biomaterials, energy systems, preservation methods and documented research projects.</li></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">First Historical Profiles</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The opening series starts with unnamed Lomekwi toolmakers and early copper workers, then examines Tubal-cain as a named scriptural metalworker before proceeding to independently attested makers and scientific authors. We will not invent the name of the world's first craftsperson or assign a fictional calendar date to a scriptural figure.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
