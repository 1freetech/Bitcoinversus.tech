<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>QuickLogic has landed a new embedded-FPGA design win for a programmable system-on-chip aimed at battery management, precision motor control and industrial robotics.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><a href="https://ir.quicklogic.com/press-releases/detail/783/quicklogic-secures-six-figure-efpga-ip-contract-for">QuickLogic announced the contract on October 13</a>. The company says the initial deal is worth six figures and targets a 16-nanometer automotive process. License revenue is expected over the next six months, with royalty revenue possible once the customer begins production shipments.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The customer was not named, and QuickLogic did not disclose a production date or expected unit volume. That makes this a design-win story, not yet a mass-production story.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The interesting part is the architecture. Instead of using a completely fixed-function SoC, the customer is embedding programmable FPGA fabric directly inside the chip. That gives the final device hardware that can be reconfigured after the chip has been manufactured.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>BitcoinVersus.Tech previously explained the core concept in <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/field-programmable-gate-array/">Field-Programmable Gate Array</a>. Traditional FPGAs sit as separate programmable chips. An eFPGA moves that flexibility inside an ASIC or SoC, where it can be used for custom interfaces, acceleration, control logic or security without adding a separate device.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>QuickLogic says the new SoC will use its Australis eFPGA IP Generator. The tool creates a programmable logic block tailored to a customer’s power, performance and area targets. According to the company, that embedded fabric can support hardware acceleration, communications interfaces, security functions and advanced control algorithms.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That fits the applications named in the announcement. Battery-management systems and motor-control systems often need deterministic timing, specialized interfaces and control logic that can change as hardware revisions or software requirements evolve. BitcoinVersus.Tech’s <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/02/armv8-m-architecture-explained/">Armv8-M architecture explainer</a> covers the embedded-control side of these systems, while our look at <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/27/qbits-qb88xx-puts-a-robot-cerebellum-on-one-chip/">QBit’s QB88XX robotics chip</a> shows the same trend toward putting more real-time control directly into silicon.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><a href="https://www.quicklogic.com/industrial-efpga-ip/">QuickLogic’s industrial eFPGA documentation</a> says its programmable blocks can be used for motor and actuator control, sensor interfaces, edge acceleration and field-device gateways. The value proposition is simple: if a control algorithm or interface changes, the programmable portion of the SoC can potentially be updated without redesigning the entire chip.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That flexibility is especially useful in products with long lifecycles, including vehicles, industrial robots and energy systems. A full chip respin can take months and substantial engineering cost. An embedded programmable block does not eliminate every redesign, but it can give engineers more room to adapt after tape-out.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>For now, the confirmed milestone is the contract itself: a six-figure eFPGA IP design win on a 16nm automotive process. The bigger test will come later, when the customer moves from licensed IP to production silicon and royalty-generating shipments.</p>
