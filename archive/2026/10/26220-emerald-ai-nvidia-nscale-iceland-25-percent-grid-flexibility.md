<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>ICELAND, October 23, 2026 —</strong> Emerald AI says it briefly lowered electricity demand from AI server racks at Nscale’s Iceland facility by <strong>about 25% for 30 minutes</strong>, using its Conductor software alongside NVIDIA DSX power management. Announced at NVIDIA GTC Berlin on October 21, the pilot tested how an AI data center could respond to a utility-style request to reduce its load.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What the 25% Result Actually Measures</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>According to <a href="https://www.emeraldai.co/blog/emeraldai-nvidia-nscale-demonstrate-grid-responsive-ai-demo-iceland">Emerald AI’s October 21 announcement</a>, the racks reduced their instantaneous power draw, maintained the lower level for half an hour, and resumed normal operation. The event followed parameters consistent with EPRI Flex MOSAIC Class B. It used a <strong>simulated grid-demand signal</strong>; the company did not report an actual Icelandic utility dispatch.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That distinction matters: a <strong>25% temporary power reduction is not a 25% permanent improvement in data-center energy efficiency</strong>. It does not by itself establish annual electricity savings, a lower facility-wide PUE, or unchanged performance for every workload. Public details do not specify the tested rack count or the starting rack load.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">How the Power Controls Work</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Emerald Conductor coordinates workload decisions with NVIDIA’s DSX controls so electrical demand can fall for a defined interval. In practice, lower-priority computations may have flexibility to slow or shift while time-sensitive inference services need tighter performance protection. Actual service levels depend on workload, policy and the available compute headroom.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><a href="https://www.nvidia.com/en-sg/case-studies/nscale/">NVIDIA’s separate Nscale case study</a> reports a 49.2% rise in aggregate inference throughput within the existing provisioned power budget and an increase in managed GPU count from 140 to 192. Those <strong>capacity-utilization figures are a separate evaluation</strong> from the 30-minute grid-response event, so the percentages must not be combined.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Bitcoin Mining Operators Will Recognize the Approach</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Bitcoin miners have long treated electricity demand as a controllable operating variable: curtailment can be preferable when the grid is constrained or electricity prices rise. AI computing adds a different challenge because customers may depend on latency, availability and contracted throughput. The common engineering principle is <strong>dispatchable demand</strong>, not identical service requirements.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":2} --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Needs to Be Proven Next</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Longer tests, utility-supervised dispatches, published workload-performance measurements and deployment across larger numbers of racks would clarify how much reliable demand flexibility this system can offer. The Iceland result is a useful controlled demonstration, while claims about faster grid connections and lower electricity costs remain prospective until utilities and operators document those outcomes.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
