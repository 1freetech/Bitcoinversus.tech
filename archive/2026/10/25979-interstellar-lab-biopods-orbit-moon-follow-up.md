<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In January 2024, BitcoinVersus.Tech <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2024/01/04/plants-on-mars-how-interstellar-labs-biopods-are-changing-agriculture/"><strong>reported on Interstellar Lab’s BioPods</strong></a>, modular controlled-environment plant systems designed to recreate climates while recycling resources and reducing the amount of human labor needed to grow plants. At the time, the most eye-catching part of the plan was Mars. Nearly three years later, the stronger story is what happened in between: the technology has split into a family of Earth, orbital and lunar systems, won a NASA food-system competition, and picked up concrete flight partners.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The original <a href="https://twitter.com/Reuters/status/1730239008641347667">Reuters post</a> showed the terrestrial BioPod concept. The important follow-up is that Interstellar Lab has moved beyond presenting a single greenhouse shell. Its space work now includes NuCLEUS, Eden and Bloom, each aimed at a different step in the path from Earth-based cultivation to long-duration human missions.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">NASA turned the idea into a real benchmark</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In 2024, Interstellar Lab won the $750,000 U.S. grand prize in NASA’s Deep Space Food Challenge with NuCLEUS, its Nutritional Closed-Loop Eco-Unit System. NASA later described NuCLEUS as an autonomous system that can grow microgreens, vegetables and mushrooms while also sustaining insects as part of a broader astronaut food supply. The system is designed around low human intervention, controlled irrigation and multiple food outputs rather than a single crop. <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/prizes-challenges-crowdsourcing-program/nasa-challenge-winners-cook/"><strong>NASA’s follow-up on the challenge</strong></a> says that work directly shaped Interstellar Lab’s next orbital platform, Eden 1.0.</p>

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The next stop is orbit</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Eden is the smaller research-focused descendant of that work. Interstellar Lab says Eden is scheduled to fly with Vast in low Earth orbit in 2027, where the automated plant-growth chamber is intended to study how plants respond to microgravity. That matters because a future Mars greenhouse cannot simply be a terrestrial farm sealed inside a pressure vessel. Engineers need real data on growth, water behavior, nutrient delivery, lighting, disease control and automation when gravity and crew time are constrained.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Then comes the Moon</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The company’s lunar system is called Bloom. Interstellar Lab says it is working with Astrolab to place the automated greenhouse on the lunar surface, with a mission target in 2028. Its broader Mission Little Prince program plans to grow a rose in orbit, on the Moon and eventually on Mars. In other words, the company is using a symbolic plant experiment to validate increasingly demanding pieces of autonomous space horticulture.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That progression also fits the infrastructure problem we discussed in <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/15/sports-on-mars-will-come-much-later/"><strong>Sports on Mars Will Come Much Later</strong></a>: before an off-world settlement has large-scale leisure, it needs reliable food, water, power, communications and life-support systems. Plant-growth hardware is part of that survival stack, not just an agricultural side project.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The business is becoming more concrete too</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Interstellar Lab’s September 2026 update says it works on Earth with L’Oréal, Robertet and dsm-firmenich on high-value plant production. The company also says it closed a $15 million Series Seed round in June 2026, bringing total capital raised to $17 million. A new partnership with space-procurement company Nebex identified $37 million in near-term opportunities and more than $400 million over five years for plant cultivation and regenerative life-support systems. Those figures are opportunity estimates, not booked backlog or guaranteed revenue, which the companies explicitly acknowledge. <a href="https://interstellarlab.com/news/interstellar-lab-and-nebex-partner-to-open-usd400-million-in-space-life-support-demand"><strong>Interstellar Lab’s September release</strong></a> provides the current commercial and mission roadmap.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What changed from our 2024 report</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The 2024 story treated the BioPod mainly as one modular greenhouse concept with a future Mars version. The 2026 picture is more mature. BioPod is now the broader technology base. NuCLEUS is the closed-loop food-production system. Eden is the orbital research unit. Bloom is the lunar greenhouse. Mars remains the long-term destination rather than the nearest deployment.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Our 2024 report also cited a $350,000 target price for a MarsPod. We are not carrying that number forward as current pricing because the company’s 2026 disclosures used for this update do not provide a current MarsPod sales price. The more verifiable milestones today are the NASA win, the Vast orbital mission, the Astrolab lunar mission and commercial deployments on Earth.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The technology still has a long way to go before it becomes a self-sustaining farm on Mars. But compared with where the story stood in early 2024, Interstellar Lab has moved from an ambitious greenhouse concept toward a staged development path with Earth customers, NASA validation and flight hardware planned for orbit and the Moon. That is a much stronger basis for judging whether autonomous plant pods can eventually become part of a real Mars settlement.</p>
