<!-- wp:paragraph {"fontSize":"large"} --><p class="has-large-font-size"><strong>AMD plans to substantially increase CPU and GPU supply in 2027 as artificial-intelligence demand keeps pressing against advanced wafer, memory, packaging and server-manufacturing capacity.</strong> The important part is that the entire production chain has to expand together.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/amd-plans-substantially-increase-supply-2027-ceo-says-2026-10-06/">Reuters reported October 6</a> that AMD Chair and CEO Lisa Su expects the company to substantially increase supply next year. During her Taiwan visit, Su emphasized the need for more advanced wafer capacity and longer-term planning with suppliers.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">AI Demand Is Becoming a Full-System Supply Problem</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A modern AI accelerator is only one part of a rack. Large deployments also need server CPUs, high-bandwidth memory, advanced packaging, networking, power conversion, cooling and final system assembly. If one layer cannot scale quickly enough, more GPU dies alone do not guarantee more finished AI systems.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>BitcoinVersus recently covered how <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/30/hpe-lands-1-2-billion-vultr-order-for-amd-helios-ai-racks/">HPE landed a $1.2 billion Vultr order for AMD Helios AI racks</a>. Orders at that scale turn directly into physical demand for silicon, memory, interconnects, boards, racks and cooling hardware.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">Taiwan Sits at the Center of AMD’s Ramp</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>AMD has already been preparing for this bottleneck. In May, the company announced <a href="https://newsroom.amd.com/news/amd-announces-more-than-10-billion-in-taiwan-ecos/">more than $10 billion of investment across Taiwan’s technology ecosystem</a>, including advanced packaging capacity and partnerships supporting next-generation AI infrastructure.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That announcement highlighted 6th Gen EPYC “Venice” CPUs, Instinct-class accelerators and the Helios rack platform. The October comments reinforce the same strategy: enough capacity has to exist across several manufacturing stages for the roadmap to become deployed infrastructure.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">More CPUs Matter Because AI Racks Are Not GPU-Only Machines</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>AI infrastructure still depends heavily on CPUs. The host processor helps coordinate storage, networking, security, orchestration and data movement around the accelerators, so AMD’s planned CPU expansion matters alongside the GPU ramp.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>BitcoinVersus recently examined <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/27/amd-epyc-venice-nvidia-vera-server-tests/">AMD’s EPYC Venice positioning against Nvidia Vera</a>. The larger strategic point is that AMD wants to supply more of the compute stack rather than competing only for accelerator sockets.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">Wafer Capacity Is Only the First Constraint</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Advanced-node wafers are essential, but high-end AI chips still have to be packaged and paired with memory before reaching a server. Complex multi-die packages and high-bandwidth memory make packaging and memory capacity major constraints of their own.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A “chip shortage” can therefore originate at several points: wafer production, advanced packaging, substrates, HBM, board assembly or rack integration. Production planners have to secure the chain rather than simply reserve more wafers.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">The Nvidia–AMD Race Is Also Becoming a Capacity Race</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Performance still matters, but at AI-factory scale the vendor that can deliver enough complete systems on schedule gains a major advantage. A technically strong accelerator that arrives late can be less useful than a platform that can actually ship in volume.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>BitcoinVersus’ <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/07/27/nvidia-vs-amd-battle-for-ai-infrastructure-leadership/">Nvidia-versus-AMD infrastructure comparison</a> framed the competition around compute, software and systems. The next phase adds manufacturing throughput: wafers, HBM, packaging and complete rack capacity.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">What to Watch in 2027</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The key question is whether AMD can increase output across CPUs and accelerators while suppliers simultaneously expand advanced wafers, memory, packaging and system assembly. If those layers scale together, 2027 could materially increase AMD’s ability to supply complete AI infrastructure.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The semiconductor story is becoming less about one chip and more about industrial throughput. AI customers consume complete, powered, cooled and networked systems—and every layer has to arrive at roughly the same time.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
