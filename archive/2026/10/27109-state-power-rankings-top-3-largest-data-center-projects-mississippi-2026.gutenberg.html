Mississippi’s data center boom is moving at two speeds. Amazon Web Services already has five buildings operating near Canton, Compass Datacenters is building an eight-building campus outside Meridian, and AVAIO Digital is still clearing land for its ambitious project in Brandon. The largest advertised capacity is not necessarily the project closest to completion.

The three largest Mississippi campuses for which we found distinguishable, publicly reported site-level power figures are AVAIO Digital Taurus (up to 1,036 MW of proposed baseload supply), Compass Meridian (500 MW in the original utility-supply plan, now marketed as 320 MW) and Amazon’s Canton campus (341 MW in a third-party facility record). These are different kinds of figures, not three measured data-center loads. The ranking reflects published project power envelopes as of October 29, 2026; where the figures or status conflict, we explain the limits instead of treating them as settled facts.

An important omission is deliberate. xAI’s $20 billion MACROHARDRR development in Southaven is one of the state’s largest technology investments, but its headline “2 GW” figure covers computing infrastructure across Memphis, Tennessee, and Southaven, Mississippi. Neither the Mississippi Development Authority announcement nor the independent site-level project record establishes 2 GW of electrical load at the Southaven building. Giving that entire number to Mississippi would misstate the comparison. The project is discussed in more detail below.

3. Amazon Web Services, Canton — 341 MW Reported Capacity; Five Buildings Operating

Unlike many of Mississippi’s announced facilities, the Amazon Web Services campus near Canton already has equipment and occupied buildings. In June 2026, Amazon spokesperson David Ross confirmed to Mississippi Today that five buildings were operating at Canton, with plans to at least double that presence. Ridgeland was in early construction, while projects in Clinton and Vicksburg remained at the site-preparation or pre-construction stage at that time. These facilities are separate locations and their capacities should not be aggregated as a single Canton campus.

The independent datacenter.fyi site record assigns the Canton location 341 MW of power capacity, but does not provide a public critical-IT-load measurement or fully transparent underlying utility contract. Another entry in the same tracking database lists approximately 477 MW for an Amazon application associated with Canton, without establishing that figure as the present operating draw. 341 MW is therefore a reported, provisional capacity marker—not an Amazon-confirmed meter reading or a verified maximum. That uncertainty could change Canton’s order relative to Compass’s newer 320 MW design.

The company’s infrastructure expansion has direct local consequences. In August, Amazon challenged the county assessor’s valuation of its Canton buildings. The county Board of Supervisors sided with the assessor, although further court proceedings remained possible. School districts are preparing for additional property-related revenue, but the precise flow of payments is affected by incentive agreements and contested valuations.

The Madison County Economic Development Authority publishes project footage and community interviews about AWS’s arrival. Its construction-milestone video is embedded here as local, official project material.

Entergy said in its June 2026 SEC filing that it had entered large-customer agreements for Amazon facilities in Madison, Warren and Hinds counties. The contracts and exact electricity pricing are not all publicly disclosed. 2. Compass Datacenters, Meridian — 500 MW Original Supply Plan; 320 MW Current Campus Design

Compass Datacenters broke ground on an eight-building hyperscale campus in Lauderdale County in January 2025. The project was introduced as a roughly $10 billion investment, including future tenants’ hardware, with an approximately eight-year buildout. Mississippi state officials said at the announcement that Mississippi Power would supply about 500 MW. The company’s current campus portfolio, however, describes the Meridian site as a 320 MW development. That more recent published figure is the appropriate reference for the developer’s present marketed design; the 500 MW number is the larger, earlier power-supply plan, not confirmed current operating consumption.

The distinction is material: a 500 MW electricity-service envelope could support a smaller critical-IT deployment after accounting for cooling, networking, conversion losses and other infrastructure. But a company lowering its currently promoted project figure to 320 MW also raises the possibility that the final campus will be different from the proposal unveiled in 2025. We do not combine 500 and 320 MW, or describe them as two separate Mississippi projects.

Compass itself publishes a short film about Meridian’s transition from a rail hub to a technology center. It is more directly relevant than generic server footage, and appears on the developer’s official Meridian listing.

Meridian’s project has moved beyond the initial announcement. The Mississippi Development Authority said on April 22, 2026 that Corderill LLC plans to invest at least $100 million in equipment as a tenant at the Compass campus, with a projected 20 direct jobs. The announcement establishes a prospective customer investment, not that the entire eight-building park is online. The Mississippi Public Service Commission previously approved a special power contract for the development in February 2025.

The state has also connected the development with workforce training. Mississippi State University’s Meridian campus participated in the groundbreaking and has highlighted the region’s expected need for engineering, information technology and skilled construction workers. The first announced equipment investment and site work are tangible milestones, but no public record reviewed here establishes 320 MW—or 500 MW—of operating load.

1. AVAIO Digital Taurus, Brandon — Up to 1,036 MW Long-Term Supply; 116 MW First Phase

The biggest advertised single-campus electrical figure belongs to AVAIO Digital Taurus in Brandon, Rankin County. The developer’s official project page lists 1,036 MW of total long-term baseload power on a 329-acre site. The components are 536 MW of proposed grid supply and 500 MW of behind-the-meter baseload generation. Those are sources of electricity for the same proposed campus, not evidence of 1,036 MW of servers or 1,036 MW of commissioned infrastructure. The behind-the-meter generating equipment, its permits and its financing are not established by the company page alone.

The developer’s September 10, 2025 construction announcement was more modest in near-term scope: a 116 MW first phase with more than 600,000 square feet of planned data center buildings. The company named Yates Construction as general contractor, said it had signed an electric-service agreement with Entergy Mississippi in August, and initially forecast first-phase completion and energization in the first half of 2027. Its current project page refers to first occupancy in August 2027. That schedule is a developer target, not a verified completed milestone.

The clearest public construction check comes from the Clarion Ledger’s October 11 report. During a September 24 site visit, the newspaper found cleared land and some equipment but no completed buildings or construction materials in view. AVAIO did not respond to the reporter’s requests for an update. The company may be performing utility or underground work that is not obvious from the surface; the observation does not establish that the project has been canceled. It does show why the 1,036 MW marketing figure should not be treated as power available for computing today.

The Mississippi Development Authority described the project as a $6 billion investment and approved its data center tax incentive. That sum represents the developer’s announced prospective investment, not confirmed construction expenditure. Officials and residents have also raised questions about the cost of grid improvements, water usage, jobs and public incentives. Future progress should be judged by dated construction evidence, the scale of the enforceable electricity contract, generation permitting and actual tenants—not the full theoretical power stack.

Why xAI’s Southaven Project Is Missing From the Power Ranking

No discussion of Mississippi’s largest technology projects can ignore xAI’s MACROHARDRR site in Southaven. The governor announced a planned investment exceeding $20 billion on January 8, 2026, describing a refurbished building near the company’s power-generation property and its other AI infrastructure just across the Tennessee state line. The announcement said the new facility would help the entire xAI Colossus compute cluster approach 2 GW; the company’s regional facilities page separately describes its combined Memphis and Southaven campuses as exceeding 2 GW.

That regional figure cannot be assigned to the Mississippi facility. Southaven’s gas-turbine site is also distinct from its computing halls: electrical generating capacity is not proof of the same amount of installed IT load. Government air permits and legal disputes concerning turbines must be evaluated on their own terms. The site-specific public record has not identified an independently documented electric-service MW value for MACROHARDRR. Without one, placing the site at #1 solely because the wider cluster is called “2 GW” would repeat the same comparison error these rankings are meant to prevent. BitcoinVersus.Tech previously reported on the 2 GW expansion; this ranking makes the geographic limitation explicit.

What the Mississippi Power Numbers Tell Us

These projects are at different stages. Canton has operating buildings, but its reported 341 MW is not an independently confirmed meter reading. Compass has a tenant commitment and a power agreement, but the original 500 MW supply figure differs from the company’s current 320 MW description. AVAIO advertises a 1,036 MW long-term electricity supply plan, while the first build is just 116 MW and recent reporting found no buildings at the site. Mississippi’s largest investment announcement, xAI, lacks a reliable standalone Southaven computing-load figure and therefore cannot be ranked on the same basis.

A megawatt measures an instantaneous rate; a megawatt-hour measures energy over time. If a future data center maintained an actual draw of 500 MW for 24 hours, it would consume 12,000 MWh, or 12 GWh. That hypothetical calculation says nothing about the current draw at Compass’s uncompleted Meridian campus. Likewise, a proposed generator rated for 500 MW does not establish 500 MW of available AI chips, and a 341 MW listed power capacity is not necessarily 341 MW of critical IT demand.

For comparison, our Minnesota report distinguishes Google’s proposed campus consumption from separate wind and battery figures, while the Michigan ranking separates utility-service contracts from speculative expansions. Mississippi’s next meaningful update will depend on site-specific utility figures for xAI and AWS, a dated construction milestone at Taurus, and confirmation of Compass’s final Meridian configuration.
