<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Bitcoin’s most revealing quotes of 2026 have not all been about price. They have been about self-custody, energy, mining, payments, institutional finance, physical infrastructure, open-source development, monetary policy and the limits of prediction itself.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This report expands our earlier <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/15/top-7-bitcoin-mining-quotes-2026/"><strong>7 Bitcoin Mining Quotes That Stand Out in 2026</strong></a> into a broader <strong>21 → 1 countdown</strong>. The ranking is BitcoinVersus.Tech editorial judgment based on clarity, explanatory value, relevance to Bitcoin in 2026 and likely staying power. A forecast, policy argument or personal interpretation is identified as such rather than presented as fact.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The selections cover public statements and interviews published through October 15, 2026. Short quotations preserve the speakers’ wording while the surrounding analysis separates verifiable technical or historical facts from advocacy, forecasts and opinion. Every ranked person is paired with a directly relevant video, social or podcast embed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">21. NG Zhang — Speed matters before scale</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“I think at the very beginning, it's all about the time to market.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Canaan founder NG Zhang was looking back at the race to build and ship the first Avalon ASIC hardware. The lesson still holds in 2026: a strong chip design creates no mining revenue until it is fabricated, delivered, installed and hashing. In mining, execution speed is part of engineering because hardware economics can change while a machine is still moving from design to deployment.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">20. Curtis Harris — Mining has to work locally</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“You can't fake local.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compass Mining’s Curtis Harris compressed a difficult operating problem into four words. Mining facilities exist in real communities with neighbors, jobs, noise, roads, substations and local governments. A power contract does not automatically create community acceptance; operators still have to show up, explain what they are building and address the effects around the site.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">19. Joe Consorti — Market price and perceived value can diverge</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“Bitcoin right now is trading at a massive discount to where it should be because the market doesn't understand what it is yet.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Consorti made the argument during Bitcoin’s February selloff. This is his valuation opinion, not an independently measurable fair value. The useful part of the quote is the distinction between a market price and an investor’s assessment of long-term monetary value: the two can diverge, and the thesis is only proven if later evidence supports it.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">18. Matt Corallo — Bitcoin benefits from many builders</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“Just having one company building the bitcoin payments platform is less likely to succeed than everyone going out and building.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Bitcoin developer Matt Corallo was discussing AI agents, payments and software competition. His point is broader than any single product: open systems improve when independent developers can experiment, fail, iterate and compete instead of waiting for one company to decide what every user needs.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">17. Arthur Hayes — A price target is only as useful as its thesis</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“I think my year-end target is around $125,000, but the exact number isn't that important.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Hayes tied the forecast to bank credit, fiscal spending, liquidity and changing monetary conditions. BitcoinVersus.Tech treats the number as his forecast, not our prediction. The stronger point is his acknowledgement that an exact target matters less than whether the macro mechanism behind it actually develops.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">16. Fred Thiel — Power is becoming strategic infrastructure</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“The only thing that matters today is owning power.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>MARA CEO Fred Thiel was deliberately reducing the mining-and-AI infrastructure race to its scarcest input. Power does not solve financing, cooling, hardware, construction or networking by itself, but energized capacity and interconnections increasingly determine which projects can exist at all.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">15. Brandon Bailey — Announcing capacity is not the same as delivering it</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“That's really the other big risk vector, which is can you actually deliver the construction?”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Bailey separates signing an infrastructure agreement from finishing the facility behind it. That distinction matters as miners reposition power portfolios for AI and high-density data centers. Megawatts on a slide deck still need transformers, cooling, redundancy, networking, financing and crews.</p>

That remains a thesis to be tested over time, not a settled law. The interview in which he made the statement was also documented by <a href="https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-05-07-bitwise-ceo-says-four-year-crypto-cycle-is-dead-as-institutional-era-takes-hold-400381">The Block</a>.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">13. Bob Burnett — Scale and efficiency are not the same thing</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“It really wasn't the survival of the fittest. It was the survival of the biggest.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Barefoot Mining founder Bob Burnett was criticizing an era when access to capital could keep large miners expanding even when smaller operators believed they had better power economics or operating discipline. His interpretation is debatable, but the distinction between financial endurance and operational efficiency is useful.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">12. Samson Mow — Persistent buyers can change the supply picture</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<p><strong>“These are buyers of last resort. They will always buy Bitcoin. They're price insensitive.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>JAN3 CEO Samson Mow used the phrase while describing large treasury and institutional buyers. “Always” and “price insensitive” are rhetorical claims rather than literal guarantees. The underlying thesis is that persistent demand for a capped asset can change market structure when the amount actually offered for sale is smaller than headline exchange balances suggest.</p>

Her position is advocacy rather than a neutral economic law, but it is specific enough to evaluate: she argues that a rules-based monetary asset can offer savers an alternative to money whose supply is politically managed.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">8. Jimmy Song — Confidence is not knowledge</h2>

<p><strong>“I don't know the consequences, and here's my claim. Neither do you.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Song wrote this during the BIP-110 debate. It stands out because the subject is uncertainty itself. Protocol decisions can have technical, economic and social effects that are difficult to model before deployment, so certainty should not substitute for testing, review and broad understanding.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">6. Adam Back — Bitcoin occupies a narrow design space</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“Bitcoin exists only in a narrow design space.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Blockstream CEO Adam Back argues that Bitcoin looks less like arbitrary software and more like a solution constrained by cryptography, incentives and distributed-systems requirements. That is partly a philosophical claim, but it goes directly to a recurring question: how much of Bitcoin’s architecture can change before the system becomes something materially different?</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">5. Caitlin Long — Self-custody changes Wall Street’s leverage</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“Wall Street will never control Bitcoin in the way that it was able to control gold.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Custodia Bank CEO Caitlin Long bases the argument largely on direct custody. Large financial institutions can influence Bitcoin markets through ETFs, derivatives and lending, but holders can still withdraw the underlying asset and control private keys themselves. That difference, in her view, limits the degree to which financial intermediaries can replicate the custody structure of gold.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">3. Jeff Booth — Self-custody is the thesis, not merely price</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“If you hold Bitcoin in self-custody, you win.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The word “win” is rhetorical; Bitcoin ownership cannot guarantee an investment outcome. Booth’s deeper argument is that Bitcoin only fully delivers on his monetary thesis when the holder is not depending on another institution’s balance sheet or permission. That shifts the focus from a price chart to ownership architecture.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">2. Michael Saylor — The 2035 “gold rush”</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“I think we're in a gold rush between now and the year 2035.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Saylor links the metaphor to Bitcoin’s issuance curve. In his September Binance interview, he said roughly 99% of the eventual 21 million BTC supply should be mined by around 2035, after which the remaining issuance extends across many decades. The “gold rush” conclusion is Saylor’s bullish interpretation. <a href="https://www.binance.com/en/blog/community/3717196676463643331">Binance’s interview summary</a> documents the argument. <h2 class="wp-block-heading">1. Jack Mallers — Bitcoin’s resistance to unilateral change</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>“Bitcoin can change the world because the world can't change Bitcoin.”</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This takes first place because it compresses Bitcoin’s rule-resistance thesis into one sentence without requiring a price target. Mallers argues that Bitcoin matters because no government, corporation or individual can simply rewrite its monetary rules on demand. Whether a reader accepts his broader conclusions or not, the quote clearly states why resistance to unilateral control remains central to Bitcoin’s identity.</p>
