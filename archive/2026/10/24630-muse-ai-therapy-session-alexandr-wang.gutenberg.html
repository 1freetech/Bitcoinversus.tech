Meta chief AI officer Alexandr Wang says one of the experiences that convinced him personal AI agents could become a major consumer product felt like “three years of therapy” compressed into a single session.

In a new David Senra interview, Wang described running an early psychoanalysis prompt through OpenClaw in February. The agent had access to his email, photos and other personal information, then pulled that context together during repeated rounds of psychological probing. Wang said the experience left him feeling unusually vulnerable — and convinced him that something important was happening.

The distinction matters: Wang did not say Meta trained Muse on the raw therapy conversation itself. What he described was an early agent using deeply personal context to perform the analysis. Meta later built Muse around more than 100 specific agent behaviors and trained and evaluated the model against those launch-blocking capabilities.

Wang said fewer than 200 people worked across Muse’s model and product teams. He argued that keeping the team small helped prevent the product from becoming a “Frankenstein” assembled from too many competing visions. That product philosophy now sits behind the broader rollout we covered when Meta pushed Muse further toward the AI frontier.

Meta’s own launch announcement says Muse runs inside a dedicated secure virtual machine and can work across the apps a person chooses to connect. Wang’s follow-up posts named Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook, Plaid, OpenTable and Spotify among the supported services. We have already seen that strategy expand into Spotify automation and DIY hardware through ESP32 and Raspberry Pi support.

The interview also reveals that product design and marketing were tightly linked. Wang openly asked, “how do we make Meta cool again?” and said memes helped the launch break through a crowded AI market. The deeper story, though, is not the memes. It is that Meta’s consumer-agent strategy was partly catalyzed by a moment in which an AI combined personal data well enough to feel psychologically revealing.
