Opinion / Basketball History

LeBron James entered his 24th NBA season in October 2026 as the league’s all-time scoring leader. But the strongest case for calling him basketball’s greatest player does not have to begin with his 2003 professional debut. It begins at St. Vincent–St. Mary High School in Akron, where a 16-year-old sophomore was already playing with the size, vision and command of someone who belonged in a much older competition. My argument is that James could plausibly have ranked among the NBA’s 100 best players on ability by spring 2001—two years before he was drafted. That is an opinion, not a historically measured NBA ranking.

The evidence is substantial. In 2000–01, James averaged 25.2 points, 7.2 rebounds, 5.8 assists and 3.8 steals in the widely cited season summaries. His Fighting Irish finished 26–1, repeated as Ohio Division III champions and placed fifth in the final USA Today national poll. The sophomore became the first 10th-grader to win Ohio Mr. Basketball and to earn USA Today All-USA first-team recognition. Sports Illustrated, the Ohio High School Athletic Association and USA Basketball preserve the record.

The teenage prospect who was already thinking like a professional

James was born December 30, 1984, so he started that sophomore season at 15 and turned 16 during it. The first surviving interviews matter almost as much as the numbers: a calm teenager described the importance of passing, defense and helping his teammates win. This was not simply a taller guard accumulating points against smaller children. He was organizing a nationally competitive team and drawing interest from college coaches and NBA scouts.

Before 2001 ended, the coverage had already spread beyond Ohio. The Sporting News’ March 26, 2001 archive listed the 6-foot-6 James on its sophomore All-America team. Its in-season figures—25.6 points, 7.5 rebounds and seven assists—differ slightly from later season summaries, an important reminder not to mix midseason reports with full-season averages. His reputation was accelerating before a national television audience could regularly watch high-school games.

A second television segment preserved from around the transition into his junior year captures the same emerging athlete: a confident ballhandler who was already discussing his future as a professional. It is contemporaneous context for his progression, not proof that he could have beaten established NBA stars in a real game.

The sophomore-season game that made the argument real

On January 13, 2001, James scored 33 points against the renowned Oak Hill Academy program. The surviving game log records 13-for-26 shooting, including five three-pointers on 11 attempts, in a narrow 79–78 loss. Oak Hill coach Steve Smith later told Yahoo Sports that James was the best sophomore he had seen. Losing that game does not diminish its significance: it was an elite teenage opponent, and James had already become the game’s defining performer.

The following summer, immediately after completing his sophomore year, James made an even louder statement at the 2001 Adidas ABCD camp. Against celebrated older prospect Lenny Cooke, a contemporary Rivals report credited James with 24 points and a game-winning shot from long distance. It was a showcase, not an NBA matchup, but it provided a revealing test against some of the strongest prospects in the country.

St. Vincent–St. Mary: four seasons of growth

James’s high-school career was an apprenticeship in full public view. His first state championship came as a freshman, the second as a sophomore, and the third as a senior. The Ohio championship archive places the first two titles in Division III and the 2003 title in Division II; St. Vincent–St. Mary lost the Division II final in 2002. Some retrospective biographies incorrectly label the early titles Division II. The official tournament records are the better authority.

Those four years also trace his transformation from an exceptional local freshman to a national celebrity. The 2001 SLAM magazine feature introduced the 16-year-old to a broader basketball readership. Sports Illustrated’s February 2002 cover and nationally televised games in his senior year amplified the phenomenon. The nearly three-year arc from his sophomore breakthrough through graduation is essential to understanding his fame, even though the biggest television exposure came later.

The 2002 Oak Hill rematch was one of those later showcases. ESPN’s archival highlights show St. Vincent–St. Mary beating Oak Hill 65–45 in December 2002. It belongs to James’s senior season and should never be presented as footage from his sophomore year.

Was he really an NBA top-100 player in 2001?

The strongest answer is plausibly, yes—if the comparison is raw basketball ability and the value of an unusually versatile wing, rather than documented production against professional defenses. At 16, James had already shown advanced court vision, rebounding instincts, transition playmaking, disruptive defense and the ability to carry a championship team. He was not yet the 6-foot-9, 250-pound veteran of later years, and no serious analysis can know how consistently he would have handled NBA physicality, defensive schemes and a full professional season.

That uncertainty matters. A teenager dominating the high-school and summer-camp circuits is not a controlled test against the NBA’s 100 best players. But the hypothesis deserves consideration because the talents that separated James—seeing passes before defenders reacted, controlling pace and scoring without sacrificing team offense—were the same skills that immediately translated when Cleveland drafted him first in 2003. As a rookie he averaged about 20.9 points, 5.5 rebounds and 5.9 assists, establishing that his skills were not merely a high-school advantage.

His final year supplied the last chapter of that preparation: three Ohio Mr. Basketball awards across 2001–03, three state championships overall, national player-of-the-year honors and a dominant performance at the 2003 McDonald’s All-American Game. It was the end of his school career but already looked like the beginning of a professional one.

Why the apprenticeship strengthens the greatest-ever argument

By October 2026, James had begun an unprecedented 24th NBA season, now with the Philadelphia 76ers. He had accumulated four NBA championships, four regular-season MVP awards, four Finals MVPs and the league’s all-time regular-season scoring record. His longevity is more than a matter of staying on a roster: he spent two decades carrying elite scoring and playmaking responsibilities across different teammates, coaches and competitive eras. That endurance complicates any argument built only around a shorter peak.

The greatest-ever discussion still has legitimate counterarguments. Michael Jordan won six championships and six Finals MVPs and produced one of basketball’s most devastating sustained scoring peaks. Bill Russell’s championships, Kareem Abdul-Jabbar’s accomplishments and other all-time greats belong in the historical discussion. Yet James’s case is uniquely broad: an extraordinary high-school prodigy became an elite rookie, a transformative superstar, an all-time scorer and a productive player into his 40s. It is why preserving performance late in his career is itself part of the story.

My verdict: LeBron James is my choice for the greatest basketball player of all time, and his case begins before the NBA. The notion that he was already top-100 NBA-caliber at 16 cannot be proven statistically. But the 2000–01 record, the 33-point Oak Hill performance, his 2001 camp breakthrough and the next quarter-century of evidence make it a defensible basketball judgment—not a fantasy. From the 2001 sophomore season to his 24th NBA campaign, the sheer duration of his excellence is the point.
