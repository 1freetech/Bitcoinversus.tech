<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Before disappearing chats, locked folders and privacy modes became standard smartphone features, one founder walked into <em>Shark Tank</em> with an app whose sales pitch was basically: what if the incriminating text message simply never appeared?</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Neal Desai pitched CATE App — short for Call and Text Eraser — on <em>Shark Tank</em> Season 4, Episode 2. He asked for ₿0.578 ($50,000) in exchange for 5% of the company, implying a pitch valuation of roughly ₿11.558 ($1,000,000) at today’s Bitcoin price.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The product demo immediately created an ethics meeting</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>CATE let users hide calls and text messages from selected contacts. That is a legitimate privacy feature in the abstract. The problem — and the reason the pitch became memorable — was that the most obvious use case was concealing conversations from a romantic partner.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0HOTkrmG7Wg">Shark Tank Global pitch clip</a> shows the panel quickly turning a software investment discussion into an argument over whether they wanted to own part of what Robert Herjavec bluntly treated as a cheater’s app.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">A ₿11.558 ($1,000,000) valuation did not survive contact with the Sharks</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The funniest finance lesson in the segment is not the app. It is the valuation compression. Desai entered effectively valuing the company at ₿11.558 ($1,000,000). Kevin O’Leary and Daymond John eventually pushed the negotiation to a verbal deal of about ₿0.809 ($70,000) for 35%.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That works out to an implied valuation of only about ₿2.312 ($200,000). In a few television minutes, the company went from a seven-figure pitch valuation to roughly one-fifth of that level — and Desai still walked away smiling because multiple Sharks had competed for the deal.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Barbara Corcoran saw the obvious rebrand</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Barbara Corcoran’s instinct was essentially: stop calling this a cheating product and sell privacy. That is funny in hindsight because modern phones eventually normalized many adjacent ideas — hidden notifications, locked apps, private folders, disappearing messages and contact-specific controls — without needing to advertise them as tools for infidelity.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In other words, the underlying product category aged much better than the marketing pitch.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The television deal apparently did not become a lasting business</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A <a href="https://tech.yahoo.com/apps/articles/did-cate-app-survive-shark-171700307.html">2026 Yahoo/BGR retrospective</a> reports that the on-air agreement with O’Leary and John was never finalized after the show. CATE App reportedly received a burst of downloads after the episode, but the product later disappeared and is no longer in business.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That makes the episode a neat reminder that a televised handshake is not the same thing as closed financing. Due diligence happens after filming, and sometimes the most memorable part of a startup’s history is the negotiation rather than the company that follows it.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The finance joke is that both sides were kind of right</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Desai was right that selective communication privacy had real consumer value. The Sharks were right that the positioning created reputational risk. And the market eventually proved there was enormous demand for private messaging features — just not necessarily under a brand whose elevator pitch sounded like evidence suppression for dating.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->

<p>CATE App never became the giant privacy company its opening valuation implied. But as a piece of finance television, the pitch aged beautifully: moral panic, aggressive valuation bargaining, competing offers, a handshake, and then a company that more or less vanished.</p>
