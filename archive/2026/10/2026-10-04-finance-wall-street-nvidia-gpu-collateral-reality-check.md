<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>NVIDIA wants AI compute to become a financeable asset class. Wall Street is not fully convinced yet.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A new <a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/nvidias-bet-that-its-chips-can-finance-ai-boom-gets-wall-street-reality-check-2026-10-01/">Reuters examination of NVIDIA’s GPU-financing push</a> finds banks, private-credit firms and infrastructure investors still assigning much shorter economic lives to AI chips than NVIDIA does. The disagreement matters because collateral value determines how cheaply the next wave of data centers can borrow.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">NVIDIA is trying to turn compute into collateral</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The strategy starts with a simple idea: if an AI accelerator keeps producing revenue for many years, it can support debt in much the same way aircraft, power plants and other long-lived infrastructure assets do.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>NVIDIA formalized that argument when it announced financing partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR. <a href="https://www.wsj.com/business/deals/nvidia-wall-street-firms-strike-ai-financing-deal-targeting-500-billion-c50377db">The Wall Street Journal’s original report on the platform</a> said the firms aimed to mobilize more than roughly ₿5.78M ($500 billion) of outside capital for AI compute.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Jensen Huang framed the effort directly in <a href="https://twitter.com/JensenHuang/status/2086934705207959965">his X post describing AI factory compute as an investable asset class</a>. The pitch is that independent capital should underwrite demand, utilization and cash flow while NVIDIA provides only selective support rather than financing the entire boom itself.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Wall Street is using a shorter clock</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The central dispute is useful life. NVIDIA argues that modern accelerators can remain productive for many years as software improves and older systems move into less demanding workloads. Credit investors are generally more conservative.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Reuters found lenders frequently modeling AI hardware around three- to four-year depreciation schedules rather than the much longer lives NVIDIA says are possible. That difference changes the entire loan structure. A GPU worth substantially less after several years provides a weaker recovery value if a borrower fails.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This is the next step in a financing question BitcoinVersus.Tech covered when <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/29/nvidia-looks-to-insurers-for-gpu-backed-ai-loans/">NVIDIA began exploring insurance around GPU-backed AI loans</a>. Insurance can transfer some default or residual-value risk, but it does not eliminate the need to decide what the hardware is actually worth after deployment.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Contracts may matter more than the chips</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Wall Street’s response is increasingly to underwrite the revenue around the GPU rather than the GPU alone. Long-term customer commitments, minimum-payment contracts, power rights and guarantees can make a financing package more attractive even when lenders remain cautious about resale value.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That distinction matters for neoclouds and smaller AI operators. A hyperscaler with a fortress balance sheet can absorb hardware depreciation. A young operator borrowing heavily against a GPU fleet has less margin for a utilization drop, a generation change or weaker rental pricing.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The AI boom is becoming a capital-structure problem</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The argument over GPU collateral is happening because the AI buildout is already too large to rely only on corporate cash flow. BitcoinVersus.Tech recently examined Bain’s estimate that <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/03/ai-70-million-bitcoin-6-trillion-revenue-2031-bain/">AI infrastructure could require a radically larger revenue base by 2031</a>. Financing cost therefore becomes part of compute cost.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If institutional investors become comfortable with GPU-backed structures, more capital can flow into data centers without every project sitting directly on a hyperscaler balance sheet. If they remain skeptical, borrowers will need stronger contracts, larger equity cushions or more support from chip suppliers.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">NVIDIA is not the only company testing this model</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The broader industry is already experimenting with alternative ways to finance expensive accelerators. BitcoinVersus.Tech reported that <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/01/broadcom-anthropic-42-billion-ai-infrastructure-financing/">Broadcom offered Anthropic a large infrastructure-financing framework tied to custom AI hardware</a>. Different suppliers are converging on the same problem: customers want far more compute than many can comfortably finance from operating cash alone.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The result is a new layer of competition. Chip companies are no longer competing only on performance per watt, software and networking. They are also competing on who can help customers secure capital, power, leases and long-term deployment economics.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The collateral test may decide how fast AI infrastructure expands</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>NVIDIA does not need Wall Street to believe a GPU lasts forever. It needs lenders to believe the combination of hardware, contracts and residual value is predictable enough to finance at scale.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>For now, the market is asking for more protection than the chipmaker’s long-life thesis alone provides. That makes the newest AI bottleneck financial rather than computational: not whether GPUs can perform the work, but whether investors can price what those GPUs will be worth after years of relentless hardware turnover.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
