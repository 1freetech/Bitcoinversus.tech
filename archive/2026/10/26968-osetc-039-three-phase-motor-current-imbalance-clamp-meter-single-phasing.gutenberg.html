OSETC.039 — Three-Phase Motor Current Imbalance. When a three-phase induction motor runs hot, trips its overload protection, vibrates or loses torque, technicians need to know whether its three line currents are reasonably balanced. Current imbalance, also called current unbalance, compares the current in each phase conductor against their average. A large difference can indicate supply-voltage inequality, a deteriorated connection, a damaged winding, a missing phase or a problem with equipment connected to the motor. This practical lesson follows motor winding-resistance testing and motor insulation-resistance testing by explaining what technicians can learn from motor current observations and how to avoid overinterpreting the results.

Three-phase current balance and why it matters

A three-phase electrical supply delivers three alternating voltages separated by approximately 120 electrical degrees. In a balanced supply feeding a symmetric motor under steady, comparable conditions, the three line-current magnitudes are expected to be close. Current imbalance means the magnitudes differ; it does not refer to how the three waveforms are phase-shifted in time. The Fluke explanation of voltage and current unbalance identifies excess heating, torque pulsation, vibration and accelerated insulation damage among the risks of unbalanced motor current.

A motor's full-load amperage (FLA) and service-factor rating provide important nameplate context, but current imbalance and loading are distinct measurements. All three currents may be equal while the motor is overloaded; conversely, one phase can be relatively high even when the motor's average current is below rated FLA. Record actual load, speed, supply conditions and the precise measurement point along with the readings.

Calculating percentage current imbalance

A common screening convention uses the maximum absolute deviation from the three-current average. Fluke presents this calculation for motor troubleshooting. It is expressed as:

Average current = (I1 + I2 + I3) / 3

% current unbalance = 100 × (largest absolute deviation from average) / average current

For example, imagine three simultaneous or near-simultaneous steady-load readings of 30 A, 36 A and 29 A. Their average is 31.67 A. The largest deviation is 36 − 31.67 = 4.33 A, giving approximately 13.7% current unbalance. This example is hypothetical and is not a measurement from a particular motor.

I1 = 30 A
I2 = 36 A
I3 = 29 A

Iavg = (30 + 36 + 29) / 3 = 31.67 A
Maximum absolute deviation = 4.33 A
Unbalance = (4.33 / 31.67) × 100 = 13.7%

Fluke's motor-maintenance measurement guidance uses a 10% current-unbalance screening guideline for many three-phase motor assessments. It is not a universal license to operate below 10% or an automatic failure verdict above it: manufacturer instructions, supply quality, duty, overload protection, temperature and the most heavily loaded phase still govern decisions. Current unbalance should be investigated alongside the motor's operating envelope, not as a standalone pass/fail metric.

Clamp-meter measurement fundamentals

A true-RMS clamp meter measures current flowing through the conductor enclosed by its jaws. For an individual motor line-current reading, it must sense the correct individual phase conductor; clamping around all three conductors together may cause the magnetic fields to cancel and does not produce three usable phase-current readings. Readings should represent the same operating state. If the load is changing rapidly, sequential handheld readings can appear different for reasons unrelated to true phase imbalance.

For safe field interpretation, use recorded readings obtained by qualified personnel with properly rated equipment and an approved method. Note the measurement location (supply side, starter load side, or a documented drive measurement point), operating speed and load, circuit condition and test instrument. If motor current changes with the process demand, a three-channel logger or appropriate motor drive analyzer can better align the measurements in time.

Voltage unbalance and winding temperature

A modest voltage inequality can cause a much larger current inequality. Nidec U.S. Motors' technical guidance, based on NEMA MG 1, explains that current unbalance during normal motor operation may be roughly six to ten times the percentage voltage unbalance. This is a rule of thumb, not a calculation guaranteed for every motor or load. The induced negative-sequence effects can increase losses and reduce available torque even while the motor continues to rotate.

Voltage imbalance should be calculated from the three line-to-line voltages at the relevant motor supply location. For instance, in a fictional example of 477 V, 480 V and 481 V, the average is approximately 479.33 V, the maximum deviation 2.33 V, and the percentage unbalance approximately 0.49%. Current and voltage imbalance use the same arithmetic form, but percentages are computed from their respective sets of values. Fluke's drive-input troubleshooting reference illustrates this distinction and cautions that significant current variation can appear even with limited voltage variation.

Single-phasing and failed connections

Single-phasing describes operation with one supply phase interrupted. Depending on the motor state and connected load, the motor may not start, may stall, or may continue running while the remaining conductors carry excessive current. In a documented running-load capture, current in one supply leg approaching zero while the other two remain active is an urgent warning that calls for following site shutdown and protection procedures. This is not the same as routine small percentage unbalance.

Fluke cautions that measuring voltage on an open or disconnected phase may be misleading because a running motor can induce or backfeed a reading. Thus, a plausible voltage indication alone does not rule out a lost phase. Broken wires, blown fuses, defective contacts and poor connections are all possibilities; do not replace a motor or defeat motor starter and overload protection without investigating the power path. Repeated thermal overload trips should also be understood alongside motor protection and coordination.

When the supply looks balanced but motor current does not

Current imbalance with relatively balanced supply voltage is a clue that the fault may lie farther downstream: a winding defect, incorrect motor terminal connection, damaged contactor pole, cable issue or an asymmetry in the driven system. The earlier winding-resistance lesson explains how to compare equivalent winding paths with appropriate low-ohm techniques; the insulation-resistance lesson describes a different class of insulation fault test. Neither test automatically proves the source of every current deviation.

A useful diagnostic record distinguishes observed phase currents, the motor's nameplate ratings, supply-voltage measurements, process load, recent maintenance and any starter faults. It also captures whether the condition persists across operating modes. Avoid the shortcut of treating a single high reading as conclusive evidence of shorted windings.

Special caution for variable-frequency drives

A motor running behind a variable-frequency drive (VFD) is not the same as an across-the-line induction motor. Drive output is typically pulse-width-modulated, making ordinary meters susceptible to misleading readings. Fluke's VFD output-measurement guidance discusses specialized instruments and filtering suitable for motor-drive signals. Never move an ordinary meter between a drive's input and output or connect/disconnect motor leads on an energized drive. Follow drive-specific manufacturer instructions, stored-energy precautions and approved work procedures.

Safety and a defensible troubleshooting record

Motor cabinets, starters, disconnects and busbars can contain hazardous electrical and mechanical energy. OSHA's electrical work-practice requirements reserve energized testing and work around exposed live parts to qualified persons under the appropriate safety controls. Before maintenance or connection changes, use the site's lockout/tagout and absence-of-voltage verification process, including management of stored and backfed energy. A measured abnormality is not authorization to bypass an interlock, move a lead, or repeatedly restart a hot motor.

  • Document the exact motor and equipment identity, nameplate FLA, service factor and how the motor was loaded.
  • Capture each phase-current reading and, where available, corresponding line-to-line voltage and power-quality data from authorized tests.
  • Compute maximum deviation from the three-current average, and state the measurement conditions and uncertainty.
  • Compare findings against manufacturer specifications, operating history, temperatures and protection events.
  • Escalate the evidence for a controlled investigation or shutdown instead of guessing which component has failed.

  • How might a running motor make an open-phase voltage check misleading?
  • Why does a VFD output require instrument and procedure considerations different from a normal sinusoidal AC feeder?

Answers: Balanced currents can all exceed a motor's allowed operating current. The example unbalance is approximately 13.7%. Changing load between sequential readings can mimic a current imbalance. The rotating machine can induce or backfeed voltage on an open leg. PWM drive outputs contain fast switching components that can mislead ordinary meters and create additional electrical hazards.
