<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>TSMC is evaluating a possible Texas investment just as one of its biggest advanced-chip customers is asking for more capacity.</strong> The timing matters because AMD CEO Lisa Su said on October 6 that the company plans to substantially increase chip supply in 2027 and needs more advanced wafer capacity over the next several years.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>TSMC has not announced a Texas fab. The company is only evaluating the possibility, according to <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/tsmc-evaluates-potential-texas-investment-sources-say-2026-09-30/">Reuters reporting from September 30</a>, and TSMC declined to comment on that report. That makes Texas a serious possibility, not a committed project.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">AMD Is Asking For More Advanced Wafers Now</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The newest demand signal came from AMD. Lisa Su said the company expects to increase supply substantially in 2027 as AI demand continues to grow and that AMD is planning capacity several years ahead. She is meeting TSMC in Taiwan to discuss expanding production capacity for CPUs and GPUs, according to <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/amd-plans-substantially-increase-supply-2027-ceo-says-2026-10-06/">Reuters on October 6</a>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That does not mean AMD is asking TSMC to build in Texas specifically. It does show why TSMC is under pressure to add more leading-edge capacity somewhere. Advanced AI processors increasingly depend on cutting-edge wafer fabrication and tightly integrated packaging, and demand is arriving faster than new fabs can be designed, equipped, qualified, and ramped.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">Arizona Is Already A $265 Billion Commitment</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Texas would come on top of a much larger U.S. manufacturing buildout already underway in Arizona. TSMC’s Arizona program has expanded from an original $12 billion project to a planned $265 billion investment spanning advanced logic fabs, advanced packaging, and research and development.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The first Arizona fab began high-volume N4 production in late 2024. The second is targeted for N3 production in the second half of 2027, while later facilities are planned around N2, A16, and newer technologies. That matters because the capacity AMD wants is not generic semiconductor capacity. The bottleneck is increasingly at the most advanced nodes and the packaging systems around them.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">A Texas Fab Would Put TSMC Closer To Another AI Cluster</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Texas already contains a dense semiconductor and AI infrastructure ecosystem. TSMC operates a design center in Austin, while Samsung has a major semiconductor presence in the state and large AI data-center projects continue to expand across Texas.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>BitcoinVersus.Tech recently covered <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/03/musk-confirms-tsmc-talks-terafab-chip-factory/">Elon Musk confirming discussions with TSMC around the Terafab project</a>. Those talks are separate from the reported Texas investment evaluation, but together they show how quickly Texas is becoming part of the conversation around future AI-chip manufacturing capacity.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">The Bottleneck Is More Than Just Wafer Starts</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Adding a fab solves only part of the supply problem. Leading-edge chips also require lithography, masks, materials, power delivery, packaging, testing, and enormous amounts of supporting facility infrastructure. BitcoinVersus.Tech has been tracking that stack through developments such as <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/01/asml-tsmc-12-inch-photomasks-high-na-euv-half-field/">ASML and TSMC’s work on larger photomasks for High-NA EUV</a> and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/25/taiwan-breaks-ground-on-tsmc-advanced-packaging-hub/">Taiwan’s new TSMC advanced-packaging hub</a>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That is why a new U.S. fab cannot be evaluated only by its cleanroom square footage. The more important question is whether the surrounding equipment, packaging, materials, utilities, workforce, and supplier ecosystem can ramp at the same speed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" style="font-family:monospace">Texas Is Still A Maybe — Demand Is Not</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>For now, the Texas story should stay in the conditional tense. TSMC has not announced a site, investment amount, construction schedule, or process technology for a Texas fab.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>AMD’s demand signal is much firmer. The company wants substantially more chip supply beginning in 2027 and is already talking with TSMC about how to secure it. Whether that capacity ultimately comes from Arizona, Taiwan, Texas, or a combination of sites, the direction is clear: advanced wafer capacity remains one of the most valuable constraints in the AI hardware supply chain.</p>
