OSEEC.024 — Control-Circuit Voltage Drop, Contactor Coil Pickup, and Transformer Regulation. A properly sized industrial control transformer can still supply inadequate voltage to a distant relay or contactor coil when the circuit energizes. Conductors, connections, the transformer’s internal impedance and other starting loads all affect the voltage that actually reaches the device. These effects matter in motor-control panels because an undervoltage coil may fail to pull in, chatter, overheat or release unexpectedly. This reference develops the control-transformer sizing, motor-control engineering and ladder-diagram troubleshooting lessons into a quantitative study of the voltage available at the load.

What is control-circuit voltage drop?

Voltage drop is the reduction in electrical potential along the source-to-load path when current flows through impedance. Even a sound conductor has resistance; switches, connectors and the output impedance of a transformer can add to the effect. The idealized relationship is ΔV = I × R, where ΔV is the conductor voltage drop in volts, I is current in amperes and R is the total resistance of the current-carrying loop in ohms. For an alternating-current control circuit, reactance and the phase relationship between current and voltage also matter, so this equation is an introductory approximation, not a complete AC design model. For foundational terms, review voltage, current and resistance and transformer turns ratio and isolation.

The important quantity is voltage measured or calculated at the coil terminals during the relevant operating event—not merely the transformer's unloaded secondary nameplate rating. A 120 V supply with nearly 120 V measured at no load may sag during a contactor's pull-in interval. A complete engineering analysis separates the source sag from the drop along the outbound and return wiring.

Why contactor coils demand more current at pickup

A conventional magnetic AC contactor uses an electromagnet to close its armature. The winding can draw substantially more apparent power during inrush than after the armature seats and the contactor is sealed. The actual values depend on the model, frequency and coil electronics. Schneider Electric explains that industrial control transformers are sized for continuous load while also tolerating brief high inrush, and that selection must consider both the inrush VA and sealed VA of starter or contactor coils.

If the source and conductors cannot supply the required pickup voltage, the armature may not close fully. Under some conditions it can oscillate, causing audible chatter, repeated switching and extra heating. This is a functional and safety problem, not merely a performance statistic. Schneider Electric's low-coil-voltage warning identifies potential coil damage, main-contact wear and unstable operation. Actual operating ranges must be obtained from the specific device: a voltage percentage accepted by one coil family is not a universal specification.

Worked example — a 120 V control circuit

Consider a fictional, illustrative 120 V AC coil labeled 240 VA inrush. The current estimate at rated voltage is I ≈ S/V = 240 VA ÷ 120 V = 2.0 A. Suppose the combined resistance of the supply and return conductors is 0.8 Ω. At this assumed current, the approximate conductor drop is 2.0 A × 0.8 Ω = 1.6 V. If the control transformer happens to provide 114 V during the same inrush interval, the approximate coil-terminal voltage is 114 − 1.6 = 112.4 V, or 93.7% of the 120 V nameplate rating.

Illustrative conditions only
Rated coil voltage:     120 V AC
Coil inrush apparent power: 240 VA
Approx. inrush current: 240/120 = 2.00 A
Round-trip conductor resistance: 0.80 Ω
Approx. cable voltage drop: 2.00 × 0.80 = 1.60 V
Transformer output during inrush (assumed): 114 V
Approx. coil voltage: 114 − 1.60 = 112.40 V
Ratio to rated voltage: 112.4/120 × 100 = 93.67%

This arithmetic is an initial screening calculation. The 2 A value is estimated at rated voltage and will not necessarily remain constant as voltage changes. AC power factor, the coil's inductive impedance, wire temperature, terminals, switching sequence and the transformer’s own transient response may change the actual result. Compare the predicted or captured voltage with the applicable manufacturer's minimum pickup or operating range rather than declaring the circuit acceptable from one percentage alone.

Worked example — why a 24 V coil is sensitive to resistance

Now imagine a separate fictional 24 V AC coil drawing 96 VA at pickup. Its rated-voltage current estimate is 96 ÷ 24 = 4 A. In a hypothetical 1 Ω round-trip conductor path, the simplified voltage drop would be 4 V. With an ideal 24 V source held constant, that leaves approximately 20 V at the coil, or 83.3% of its nominal voltage. Real coil current and the AC voltage phasors require a more detailed calculation, but the example makes the trend clear: an identical drop consumes a much larger fraction of a low-voltage circuit's available margin.

Increasing cable length increases resistance, while increasing conductor cross-sectional area usually decreases it. Schneider Electric's long-distance contactor-control guidance explicitly discusses coil voltage drops over extended remote-control wiring. Its TeSys technical catalog, in the section on long-distance remote control, relates allowed cable distance to coil inrush VA, wire size and control voltage. Those tables are tied to specified equipment assumptions and must not be generalized to every installation.

Transformer regulation and the supply-voltage budget

Voltage regulation describes the change in a transformer's secondary voltage between different loading conditions. An unloaded control transformer may be near the nominal secondary rating, yet its output can decline during the inrush of several coils. Transformer internal resistance, leakage reactance and source voltage variation determine the available voltage. This is why manufacturer regulation tables are more useful than selecting transformer VA from the continuous current alone.

Schneider Electric's control-transformer selection overview instructs designers to calculate continuous sealed VA and peak simultaneous inrush VA, then compare the requirements against a regulation table. Its example columns address output percentages such as 95%, 90% and 85% of rated secondary voltage; those values are selection conditions, not generic permission to operate all contactors at low voltage. The manufacturer also cautions against selecting its 85% secondary-voltage column for routine starting. Chatter, nuisance dropout and failure diagnosis

When a contactor buzzes, fails to pull in or repeatedly drops out, consider several distinct fault families: inadequate upstream control voltage; transformer inrush capacity; long or undersized conductors; loose or degraded contacts; incorrect coil selection; electrical interlocks; faulty command logic; and mechanical or armature defects. Do not interpret a single sound as proof that the transformer is undersized. The control-circuit fault-finding chapter explains how control logic and contact state can produce similar symptoms.

If the measured supply voltage is stable but the coil voltage falls during pickup, the wiring, switching contacts or intermediate connections deserve investigation. If the transformer secondary itself collapses under inrush, review its primary supply, nameplate, actual device burdens, regulation data and simultaneous switching conditions. If coil voltage is within the manufacturer's operating range yet the equipment still chatters, mechanical or coil-specific faults may be plausible. Environmental effects and power-quality disturbances can complicate interpretation.

Engineering review and electrical safety

An engineer should document source voltage, transformer catalog and tap settings, all coil VA ratings, peak simultaneous inrush sequence, circuit length, conductor material, estimated loop impedance, connection condition and actual device operating limits. For an existing installation, review approved historical test records and any instrument captures made by authorized personnel; never use a steady-state no-load reading as proof that a transient pickup event is within tolerance.

Measurements inside energized motor-control enclosures can expose workers to electric shock and arc-flash hazards. Follow the employer's electrical-safety program and lockout/tagout procedures. Where energized diagnostic measurements are justified, qualified personnel must use an approved work procedure, the correct rated instruments and personal protective equipment. This lesson does not authorize live probing, bypassing interlocks, overriding safety controls or changing wiring.

Knowledge check

  • Why can a 120 V coil receive materially less than 120 V at the terminals even when its transformer is rated 120 V?
  • Why must a control-transformer design account for both sealed VA and inrush VA?
  • In the fictional 120 V example, what approximate conductor drop was obtained from a 2 A current and 0.8 Ω loop resistance?
  • Why does the same absolute voltage drop consume more voltage margin on a 24 V circuit than on a 120 V circuit?
  • Why are manufacturer-specific pickup thresholds and regulation tables necessary?

Answers: Conductors and equipment have impedance, while the loaded transformer voltage can sag. The steady load determines continuous capacity, whereas magnetic-coil pickup creates a short transient burden. The computed conductor drop is 1.6 V. Four volts is 16.7% of 24 V but only 3.3% of 120 V. Finally, different coil designs and transformer types have different characteristics, so one generic threshold cannot establish compliance or reliable operation.
