Optical network protection keeps traffic alive when a fiber, amplifier, line card, node, or path fails. The engineering problem is not simply “add a second fiber.” A resilient design must decide what layer detects the failure, what layer switches traffic, how quickly the switch happens, whether the alternate path is truly diverse, what failures remain common to both paths, and how the network returns to normal after the fault clears.

This lesson follows OSFOEC.008: ROADM and Optical Line Systems. ROADMs and line systems provide the photonic infrastructure. OSFOEC.009 explains how engineers protect services across that infrastructure using OTN protection switching, optical-path diversity, automatic protection switching, control-plane restoration, and carefully defined failure domains.

Protection and Restoration Are Different

Protection normally pre-arranges a backup resource before failure. The alternate path, protection entity, or duplicate signal is already defined, so switching can happen quickly and predictably. Restoration typically computes or selects a new route after a failure, often using a control plane or network-management system.

Protection usually trades extra capacity for speed and determinism. Restoration can use network capacity more efficiently, especially in large mesh networks, but may require more signaling, path computation, resource checks, and convergence time.

A network can use both. For example, a critical ODU service might use preplanned 1+1 protection against a local span failure while the optical control plane also retains the ability to restore around larger multi-node failures.

ITU-T G.873.1 Defines OTN Linear Protection

ITU-T G.873.1 defines automatic protection switching and linear protection behavior for Optical Transport Network services at the ODU level. The in-force recommendation and its amendment cover 1+1, 1:n, and 1:1 protection forms for protected ODU entities.

The architecture uses two important functions. The bridge at the head end places normal traffic onto the working transport entity and, when required, the protection transport entity. The selector at the tail end chooses which received copy becomes the service output.

1+1 Protection

In 1+1 protection, one working service has one dedicated protection transport entity. The head end permanently bridges the same traffic onto both working and protection paths. The tail end continuously receives both and selects the better or currently preferred signal.

  • Traffic is duplicated continuously.
  • The protection path is dedicated to one working service.
  • The head end does not need to establish a new bridge at failure time.
  • Switching can be very fast because the tail end mainly changes its selector.
  • The protection resource cannot normally be used efficiently for unrelated high-priority traffic while both copies are active.

1+1 is conceptually simple and attractive for critical services where recovery speed matters more than capacity efficiency.

1:1 Protection

In 1:1 protection, one working entity has one dedicated protection entity, but the protection path is not permanently carrying the same service in the same way as 1+1. The protection entity can potentially carry lower-priority extra traffic when it is not needed for protection, depending on implementation and policy.

When protection is required, the head end bridges the protected traffic onto the protection entity and the tail end selects it. That requires coordination, so 1:1 involves more switching logic than simple permanent-bridge 1+1.

1:n Protection

1:n protection lets one protection entity protect multiple working entities. Only one of the protected working services can normally consume that shared protection resource at a time.

This improves capacity efficiency compared with dedicating one protection entity to every service. The tradeoff is contention: if two protected working entities fail simultaneously, priority rules decide which service gets the shared protection resource.

Physical Diversity Comes Before Protocol Diversity

No protection protocol can save a service if the working and backup resources share the same failure. Two fibers in the same cable are not diverse against cable cuts. Two cables in the same conduit are not diverse against excavation. Two routes entering the building through the same handhole can share the same last-mile risk.

Engineers should document shared risk groups: conduit, bridge crossing, utility pole line, cable sheath, splice enclosure, hut, amplifier site, ROADM node, power plant, cooling system, management network, software domain, and human-maintenance process. Logical diagrams that show two colored lines are insufficient unless those lines map to actual physical routes.

Protection Switching Needs Defect Triggers

Automatic protection switching starts when the system detects a condition serious enough to justify a switch. Depending on the layer and vendor, triggers may include loss of signal, loss of frame, high bit-error rate, signal fail, signal degrade, trail signal fail, equipment failure, or a manually initiated command.

Trigger thresholds must be chosen carefully. A threshold that is too sensitive can cause unnecessary oscillation between paths. A threshold that is too tolerant can leave traffic on a badly degraded path for too long.

Signal Fail Versus Signal Degrade

Signal fail represents a severe defect where service is effectively unusable or the trail is lost. Signal degrade represents quality that has crossed a configured degradation threshold while the service may still be passing traffic.

Fail conditions typically carry higher protection-switch priority than degrade conditions. This prevents a mild quality problem from blocking recovery for a service that has completely failed.

Automatic, Manual, and Forced Switches

  • Automatic switch: initiated by detected network defects.
  • Manual switch: operator requests movement to a healthy alternate path, usually with lower priority than severe automatic defects.
  • Forced switch: operator explicitly forces traffic onto a selected protection path, typically overriding normal preference.
  • Lockout of protection: prevents the protection entity from being selected, commonly during maintenance or testing.

Every operator command should have a documented priority relationship with automatic defects. Otherwise a maintenance command can accidentally block protection during a real failure.

Revertive and Non-Revertive Operation

After the working path recovers, the network needs a policy for what happens next.

  • Revertive: traffic eventually returns to the preferred working path after it is proven stable.
  • Non-revertive: traffic remains on the currently selected healthy path until another event or operator action changes it.

Revertive operation restores the intended capacity layout and frees the protection resource for future failures. Non-revertive operation avoids an additional service switch after recovery. The correct choice depends on operational policy, capacity planning, and service requirements.

Wait-to-Restore Prevents Flapping

In revertive systems, wait-to-restore holds traffic on the protection path for a defined interval after the working path appears healthy. This helps prove that the fault has really cleared before traffic is switched back.

Without WTR, a marginal fiber, unstable amplifier, dirty connector, or intermittent line card can create repeated working-to-protection-to-working oscillation. Flapping may cause more service disruption than simply remaining on the protection path while technicians repair the root cause.

Hold-Off Timer

A hold-off timer intentionally delays a protection action for a short period to give a lower-layer mechanism time to recover first. Multi-layer networks can otherwise react to the same fault at several layers simultaneously.

Example: if the photonic layer can switch around a line failure in tens of milliseconds, an OTN or packet layer may use a hold-off interval so it does not begin a larger reroute for a fault the optical layer is already repairing.

Unidirectional and Bidirectional Switching

For bidirectional services, protection can be coordinated in different ways. In unidirectional switching, each direction can select independently. A failure affecting only A-to-B may cause only that direction to switch. In bidirectional switching, endpoints coordinate so both directions use corresponding working or protection entities.

Bidirectional switching requires signaling or a protection communication channel so both endpoints agree on state. The advantage is operational symmetry; the tradeoff is additional coordination logic.

Protection Versus Control-Plane Restoration

An optical control plane can maintain topology, resource, and connectivity information and compute alternate paths after a failure. That is fundamentally different from a dedicated 1+1 protection pair.

  • Dedicated protection: resources are reserved in advance; switching is predictable and fast.
  • Shared protection: protection capacity is reserved but shared among multiple services under defined contention rules.
  • Dynamic restoration: the control plane calculates or signals a new path from currently available resources after failure.

Dynamic restoration can route around more complex failures than a single fixed protection pair, but restoration time depends on failure detection, topology convergence, path computation, signaling, resource availability, and optical feasibility.

Photonic Protection and OTN Protection Protect Different Things

A photonic protection switch can move an entire optical spectrum or wavelength path between fiber routes. OTN protection can protect an ODU service electrically across transport resources. Higher layers such as Ethernet, MPLS, Segment Routing, or IP can also reroute traffic.

Choosing the layer determines the scope of the event. A photonic switch can restore many wavelengths at once, but one bad photonic decision can move many services simultaneously. OTN protection can protect individual transport clients with more granularity. IP restoration can select entirely different packet paths but may converge more slowly and may not preserve strict latency or deterministic bandwidth.

Layer Coordination Matters

Multi-layer resilience can become counterproductive if every layer reacts immediately to the same fault. The optical layer, OTN layer, Ethernet layer, and IP/MPLS layer may all detect failure independently.

Engineers should define a recovery hierarchy. One common strategy is to allow the fastest lower-layer protection action first, use hold-off timers at higher layers, and let packet rerouting respond only if the lower layer cannot restore service. Other networks deliberately favor packet restoration for operational simplicity. The design should be explicit, not accidental.

Sub-50 ms Is a Target, Not a Magical Guarantee

Carrier transport designs often target protection switching on the order of tens of milliseconds, and many systems advertise sub-50 ms protection for supported scenarios. End-to-end service interruption can still be longer because failure detection, propagation, FEC behavior, client equipment, packet buffering, protocol timers, and downstream convergence contribute additional time.

Measure the application-visible interruption during commissioning. Do not assume a transport-layer switch time is identical to what the customer experiences.

Protection Paths Must Be Capacity-Feasible

A backup path that exists topologically may not have enough usable capacity when failure occurs. Engineers need to know whether protection capacity is dedicated, shared, preemptable, or merely hoped to be available.

In DWDM networks, restoration also has optical constraints: spectrum availability, channel spacing, amplifier loading, OSNR, dispersion, nonlinear margin, wavelength continuity where required, ROADM degree connectivity, transponder reach, and regeneration placement. A route can be physically connected yet optically infeasible for a high-rate coherent service.

Failure Domains Must Be Modeled Explicitly

  • Single fiber strand.
  • Fiber pair or ribbon.
  • Cable sheath.
  • Conduit or duct bank.
  • Splice enclosure.
  • Building entrance.
  • ROADM node.
  • Amplifier hut.
  • Line card or shelf.
  • DC power feed.
  • Cooling plant.
  • Software controller cluster.
  • Management network.
  • Common maintenance window.
  • Shared carrier or third-party infrastructure.

Protection design is strongest when each important service has a documented answer to: Which single failures can still take down both working and protection paths?

Route Diversity Must Be Verified, Not Assumed

Route-diverse circuits should be validated using provider route records, GIS/KML information where available, entrance-facility documentation, splice diagrams, conduit records, and field inspection. Two carrier order numbers do not prove two physical paths.

Pay special attention to the first mile, building entrance, metro aggregation site, bridge crossings, railroad crossings, and river crossings. Networks that appear geographically diverse across hundreds of kilometers can still share one critical duct for the final 200 meters.

Protection Priorities and Contention

Shared protection requires a priority model. If several services request the same protection resource simultaneously, the system needs deterministic rules for who wins.

Mission-critical services may receive higher protection priority than best-effort services. Extra traffic carried on an idle protection channel is commonly preemptable. Engineers should verify that the configured priority hierarchy matches the business-impact classification, not merely the order in which services were provisioned.

Protection State Must Be Observable

Operations teams need clear visibility into working/protection state, active defects, switch reason, requested command, current selector state, APS messages, WTR timer, route identity, and whether the service is operating with reduced redundancy.

A protected service that has switched successfully is not “healthy” in the same sense as a fully redundant service. It is carrying traffic, but its next failure may cause an outage. NOC alarming should distinguish service down from service up but protection consumed.

Maintenance Can Defeat Protection

Many major outages occur when maintenance removes one path while the supposedly protected path is already impaired or shares the same maintenance domain. Before planned work, confirm protection availability, active path state, open defects, route diversity, and any lockout or forced-switch commands left behind from previous work.

A maintenance plan should state which failures remain survivable during the maintenance window. If redundancy is intentionally reduced, the change ticket should make that degraded condition explicit.

Acceptance Testing Must Inject Real Failures

A configuration screen showing “Protection Enabled” is not proof. Commissioning should create controlled failures and verify actual behavior.

  1. Confirm working and protection physical routes.
  2. Confirm normal traffic is on the intended working path.
  3. Record baseline BER, optical power, OSNR, latency, and alarms.
  4. Simulate or create an approved working-path failure.
  5. Measure service interruption time from the client perspective.
  6. Verify the tail-end selector or path state changes as designed.
  7. Verify alarms identify the actual root failure and protection state.
  8. Confirm protection capacity is not contended unexpectedly.
  9. Restore the working path.
  10. Verify WTR and revertive/non-revertive behavior.
  11. Test a protection-path failure while traffic is on the working path.
  12. Test operator manual switch and clear commands.
  13. Verify lockout behavior only under controlled maintenance conditions.
  14. Repeat for each required failure domain in the acceptance plan.

Measure Service Disruption, Not Just Device State

Protection testing should use continuous traffic or a dedicated analyzer to measure frame loss, packet loss, bit errors, sequence errors, and exact service interruption time. Optical equipment may report a 30 ms switch while the client sees 80 ms because of FEC reacquisition, PCS synchronization, Ethernet link behavior, or downstream equipment timers.

Common Engineering Failure Patterns

  • Both paths fail from one backhoe: working and protection routes share conduit or cable sheath.
  • Protection path exists but cannot carry the service: capacity or optical feasibility was never validated.
  • Traffic oscillates after repair: WTR is missing, too short, or the working path remains unstable.
  • Higher layers reroute before optical protection finishes: hold-off and multi-layer recovery policy are misaligned.
  • Service remains on protection indefinitely: non-revertive policy is configured or WTR/reversion is blocked.
  • Manual switch fails during maintenance: higher-priority defect, lockout, or forced command already owns the protection state.
  • Two supposedly diverse circuits fail together: hidden shared-risk group exists at building entrance, splice case, PoP, power, or carrier infrastructure.
  • Protection works but customers still see outage: client equipment or upper-layer protocols require longer reacquisition than transport switch time.

Engineering Exercise

Design protection for a 400G coherent service between Data Center A and Data Center B. The primary route is 420 km through ROADMs R1-R4. A secondary route is 510 km through ROADMs R5-R9. Both routes currently enter Data Center B through the same underground duct for the last 300 meters.

Identify the shared-risk problem, then redesign the final approach so the protection route has a separate entrance. Choose between 1+1 OTN protection and dynamic control-plane restoration. Explain your choice in terms of switch speed, capacity reservation, optical feasibility, failure coverage, and operational complexity. Define the failure injections you would require before accepting the design.
