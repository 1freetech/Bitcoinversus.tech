<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>OSFOTC.013 — Fiber Splice Tray and Closure Acceptance.</strong> A fusion splice can test well on the bench yet become unreliable if its fiber is pinched, routed too tightly or left unsupported in a splice tray. This lesson focuses on one field skill: inspecting and documenting a completed fiber splice tray or closure before it is accepted and sealed. It follows <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/25/osfotc-012-fiber-connector-endface-inspection-iec-pass-fail/">OSFOTC.012 on connector end-face inspection</a> and builds on <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/08/osfotc-006-fiber-fusion-splicing-strip-clean-cleave-splice-protect-test/">OSFOTC.006 on fusion splicing</a>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2>Learning objectives</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul><li>Identify a protected splice and its tray-routing requirements.</li><li>Inspect fiber slack, strain relief, bend radius, labels and closure seals before handoff.</li><li>Distinguish a visual acceptance check from measured optical-loss certification.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2>1. What the closure protects</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A splice closure or splice tray manages the delicate glass fiber and its protective sleeve after fusion splicing. It is designed to keep fibers routed within the manufacturer's permitted bend radius, prevent pull forces from reaching bare glass, protect against moisture and contamination, and maintain a traceable map between incoming and outgoing fibers. A tidy appearance alone is not evidence of acceptable optical performance.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2>3. A practical pre-close inspection</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul><li><strong>Confirm identity.</strong> Check work order, cable ID, tube and fiber color codes, closure type, tray number and splice plan before accepting the assembly.</li><li><strong>Verify splice protection.</strong> Check that each splice sleeve is fully seated in the approved holder, with no exposed unprotected bare glass.</li><li><strong>Check routing.</strong> Trace the fibers around the tray, checking for pinches, sharp bends, crossing under fasteners, trapped loops and excess tension.</li><li><strong>Check strain relief.</strong> Ensure the cable sheath and strength members are secured as specified so a pull on the cable does not reach the spliced fibers.</li><li><strong>Check enclosure integrity.</strong> Inspect gaskets, ports, cable seals, closure hardware and cleanliness using the manufacturer's procedure; never improvise sealing materials.</li><li><strong>Record the result.</strong> Capture approved photos, tray maps, test results, identifiers and any deviations before closure.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Follow the exact closure maker's instructions for approved fiber capacity, storage geometry, sealing, re-entry and environmental rating. For example, <a href="https://www.commscope.com/product-type/cabinets-panels-enclosures/closures-terminals/fiber-splice-closures/">CommScope's fiber splice closure resources</a> and <a href="https://www.corning.com/optical-communications/worldwide/en/home/products/fiber-optic-cable-hardware-accessories/fiber-optic-hardware/splice-closures.html">Corning's splice closure catalog</a> illustrate how closure designs differ.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2>5. Worked acceptance example</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A technician finishes a 12-fiber repair. The splice machine reported estimated losses within its own target for all twelve joints, but the tray inspection finds one fiber trapped beneath a retainer and two splice sleeves not seated fully. The closure should <strong>not</strong> be sealed as accepted. The technician follows the approved rework procedure, corrects the mechanical defects, verifies the final fiber path, and repeats any required optical tests.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The splicer's estimated loss is not a substitute for independent link acceptance. <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/12/osfotc-009-fiber-optic-link-testing-certification/">OSFOTC.009</a> explains why certification requires the correct test method, reference conditions and documented acceptance criteria. <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/osfotc-003-otdr-field-testing-launch-receive-fibers-range-pulse-width-events-dead-zones-fault-location/">OSFOTC.003</a> covers OTDR event testing when needed.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2>6. Safety and handoff</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul><li>Treat all fibers as potentially carrying invisible laser light. Never look into an unverified fiber end or use an optical microscope without appropriate safety controls.</li><li>Collect cleaved glass fragments in a dedicated sharps container and keep food and drink away from the work area.</li><li>Do not open an active network closure, underground vault or overhead enclosure without approved authorization and hazard assessment.</li><li>Log the splice map, test instrument, wavelength, acceptance limits, results, closure part number, seal inspection and final handoff.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>For foundational handling, review <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/04/osfotc-001-fiber-optic-safety-handling-inspection-cleaning-basics/">OSFOTC.001</a>. The <a href="https://www.thefoa.org/tech/ref/OSP/closures.html">Fiber Optic Association's outside-plant closure reference</a> provides additional installation context.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul><li>Why is a good estimated fusion-splicer loss insufficient to approve a sealed closure?</li><li>Name two routing or protection faults that should be corrected before closing a splice tray.</li><li>What documentation makes a repaired fiber route easier to troubleshoot later?</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->
