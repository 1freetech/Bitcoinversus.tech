A FinFET improves transistor scaling by moving the channel into a vertical silicon fin and wrapping the gate around multiple sides of that fin. Compared with a planar MOSFET, the gate gains stronger electrostatic control over the channel, which reduces source-to-drain coupling, suppresses short-channel effects, and improves off-state leakage control.

This lesson follows OSSEC.007: Short-Channel MOSFET Effects. That lesson explained why planar scaling produces DIBL, threshold-voltage roll-off, leakage, and degraded electrostatic control. FinFETs were developed specifically to improve those electrostatics.

From One-Sided Gate Control to Multi-Gate Control

In a conventional planar MOSFET, the gate controls the channel primarily from above. As gate length shrinks, the source and drain electric fields increasingly influence the channel potential. The drain can lower the source-channel barrier, producing drain-induced barrier lowering and increased off-state current.

A FinFET changes the geometry. The silicon body becomes a narrow vertical fin and the gate controls the channel along the two sidewalls and, in a tri-gate implementation, across the top surface as well. UC Berkeley research describes this multi-gate geometry as providing stronger electrostatic control and better suppression of short-channel effects than planar bulk CMOS.

Intel’s 22 nm tri-gate description similarly explains that conducting channels form on three sides of the vertical fin. The physics is straightforward: more gate-controlled surface surrounds a thinner semiconductor body, so the gate has greater influence over channel potential relative to the drain.

Fin Width Controls Electrostatic Integrity

Fin width is one of the most important electrostatic dimensions. A narrow fin lets the electric field from the gate penetrate and control more of the silicon body. If the fin becomes too wide relative to gate length, the interior of the body is less tightly controlled and short-channel behavior worsens.

NIST semiconductor-metrology material identifies gate length and fin width as critical FinFET dimensions because electrostatic control depends strongly on geometry. This is why advanced fabrication requires tight control of fin etch, sidewall profile, line-edge roughness, and final fin dimensions.

Fin Height Controls Available Channel Width

Fin height affects how much conducting sidewall area exists in the same horizontal footprint. For a simplified tri-gate FinFET, the effective channel width of one fin can be approximated as Weff ≈ 2Hfin + Wfin, where the two sidewalls contribute roughly twice the fin height and the top contributes roughly the fin width.

For multiple identical fins, a first-order estimate is Weff,total ≈ Nfin(2Hfin + Wfin). Real compact models include additional geometry, corner, parasitic, mobility, and process effects, but this approximation explains why taller fins can increase drive current without consuming the same planar width that a wider flat transistor would require.

FinFET Width Is Quantized

Planar transistor designers can often vary channel width continuously over a broad range. FinFET design is more discrete because drive strength is commonly adjusted by choosing an integer number of fins.

A one-fin device, two-fin device, and three-fin device therefore represent distinct sizing steps. This quantization affects standard-cell design, analog sizing, matching strategy, SRAM optimization, and layout. A designer cannot always request an arbitrary width and expect the physical device to realize it exactly.

DIBL Improves Because the Gate Wins More of the Electrostatic Competition

Drain-induced barrier lowering occurs when drain voltage significantly lowers the source-channel energy barrier. In a well-controlled FinFET, the gate surrounds more of the channel and the silicon body is thin, so the drain has less ability to reshape the barrier.

This does not make DIBL disappear. Gate length, fin width, source/drain geometry, junction profile, dielectric thickness, gate work function, and process variation still matter. The benefit is that FinFET architecture provides a stronger starting point for electrostatic scaling than a similarly aggressive single-gate planar device.

Subthreshold Slope and Leakage

Better electrostatic control makes the subthreshold characteristic steeper and reduces off-state leakage compared with a poorly controlled planar device at comparable scaling. A conventional thermionic MOSFET still remains subject to the familiar room-temperature Boltzmann limit near 60 mV per decade in the ideal case; FinFET geometry helps the real device approach good electrostatic behavior but does not eliminate that fundamental MOSFET limit.

Leakage also includes more than channel subthreshold current. Junction leakage, gate-related leakage, gate-induced drain leakage, punch-through paths, and other parasitic mechanisms can remain important as dimensions shrink.

Threshold Voltage With a Lightly Doped Channel

Multi-gate electrostatics allow the FinFET channel to use lower channel doping than older planar approaches that depended heavily on doping profiles to control short-channel effects. Lower channel doping can reduce random dopant fluctuation and avoid some mobility degradation associated with heavy impurity scattering.

Threshold voltage is therefore strongly engineered through gate-stack materials and metal-gate work function in addition to geometry and channel properties. This links FinFET design directly to high-k dielectric and metal-gate process integration.

Bulk FinFET Versus SOI FinFET

FinFETs can be built on bulk silicon or silicon-on-insulator substrates. SOI naturally isolates the device body from the substrate with buried oxide. Bulk FinFETs are manufactured directly in bulk silicon and require engineering at the base of the fin to control unwanted leakage paths.

UC Berkeley work on bulk FinFETs discusses punch-through stopper engineering beneath the active fin as one method for suppressing source-to-drain leakage through the substrate. The exact structure is process-specific, but the lesson is general: excellent gate control around the visible fin does not remove the need to control leakage underneath it.

Parasitics Still Matter

A FinFET can have excellent intrinsic channel electrostatics and still lose circuit performance to parasitic resistance and capacitance. Source/drain resistance, contact resistance, gate resistance, overlap capacitance, fringe capacitance, local interconnect, and self-heating all influence the usable current and delay seen by a circuit.

This is why transistor architecture cannot be evaluated only from an idealized cross-section. The complete device includes contacts, spacers, epitaxy, isolation, gate stack, local wiring, and thermal paths.

Process Variation Becomes Geometry Variation

In a FinFET, dimensions such as fin width, fin height, gate length, sidewall angle, corner rounding, and fin pitch directly affect electrical behavior. Lithography and etch variation therefore become device-physics variation.

A narrower-than-target fin may improve electrostatic control but also reduce available channel cross-section and alter resistance. A taller fin may increase effective width but complicate patterning, mechanical stability, gate fill, and parasitic control. Device engineering is therefore a multidimensional optimization rather than a search for one universally smallest dimension.

Why Gate-All-Around Comes Next

FinFETs improve control by wrapping the gate around three sides of a fin. Gate-all-around transistors extend the same idea by surrounding the channel on all sides. NIST describes GAA devices as successors to FinFETs for still smaller characteristic dimensions, while Intel’s RibbonFET uses stacked ribbon-shaped channels with gate material around them.

The progression is therefore electrostatically intuitive: planar gate control from one primary surface, FinFET control from multiple surfaces, and gate-all-around control around the full channel perimeter.
