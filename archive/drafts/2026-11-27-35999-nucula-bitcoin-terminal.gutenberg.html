Bitcoin payment infrastructure may eventually extend beyond smartphones and conventional point-of-sale systems. Nucula, an experimental open-hardware terminal developed by Dario (zeugmaster), combines a small microcontroller with near-field communication (NFC) and Cashu electronic cash. The developer demonstrated the project at bitcoin++ Berlin on October 3, 2026, and November 26 reporting estimated prototype component costs at approximately $23 per unit. The figure excludes the expenses associated with a finished commercial product.

A payment system on a microcontroller

The public hardware repository specifies an ESP32-C3-MINI-1 module, an NXP PN7160 NFC controller, an OLED interface and a detachable keypad. The four-layer printed circuit board incorporates a 40 × 40 mm antenna coil, battery charging circuitry and a USB-C connection. The separate firmware repository documents the Cashu wallet, Wi-Fi configuration and serial console.

NFC transfers small amounts of data over a short-range radio link. For a payment, compatible devices exchange token information rather than broadcasting a new transaction directly to Bitcoin miners. A detachable keypad supports variable purchase amounts, while fixed-price equipment such as vending machines could use a different input arrangement. Related open-hardware innovation is examined in our OpenSats project coverage.

The October 9 manufacturing package includes editable KiCad files and fabrication instructions. The repository identifies unfinished bench validation for revised USB and display behavior and NFC component substitutions. Passing electrical and design-rule checks does not establish reliability under repeated customer use. Watch the original developer presentation on the bitcoin++ conference website.

Cashu and the Lightning Network

Cashu uses cryptographically signed electronic tokens issued by a mint. The protocol connects issuance and redemption to Bitcoin Lightning payments. Blind signatures can prevent a mint from directly correlating a particular token issuance with its later redemption, although network metadata and implementation details can introduce other privacy risks.

A wallet can hold and transmit tokens without writing each transfer to the Bitcoin blockchain. The recipient still depends on the issuing mint to honor the tokens. The Cashu specification describes how a mint verifies proofs and tracks spent secrets. Offline token reception must not be equated with verified settlement because a recipient may need subsequent communication with the mint to confirm spendability. Our Lightning mining payouts coverage and Breez wallet SDK report examine separate approaches to Bitcoin payment infrastructure.

From prototype to commercial infrastructure

A low-cost circuit board could make dedicated payment equipment more accessible, but merchants require more than a working radio interface. They need transaction reconciliation, predictable error handling, secure storage, firmware updates and procedures for connectivity failures. Nucula’s firmware documentation warns that erasing flash can destroy stored wallet information and proofs. Physical access to the serial console is another security boundary requiring evaluation before widespread deployment.

Open designs allow independent engineers to examine and modify hardware. They do not automatically establish resistance to tampering or a mint's solvency. As our Payjoin coverage illustrates, different privacy techniques have distinct trust assumptions.

The next meaningful milestones are repeatable hardware validation, independently documented payment success rates and field trials. Nucula demonstrates a potential path toward purpose-built Bitcoin payment interfaces, but neither the component estimate nor the conference demonstration establishes commercial readiness.
